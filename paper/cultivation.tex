\begin{abstract}
Magic-state cultivation is a leading way to prepare the T states that fault-tolerant quantum computers need. Its circuits are designed with efficient simulations that replace every T gate by an S gate. This hides a loss of fault tolerance. An exact criterion reduces any cultivation circuit to one stabilizer code on its T gates and shows that current checks tolerate only half the faults their simulations promise. We identify the cause and design checks that avoid it. On a square grid, they keep the full fault distance and fail orders of magnitude less often than current checks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A fault-tolerant quantum computer needs a steady supply of T states, $|T\rangle=(|0\rangle+e^{i\pi/4}|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$, because the Clifford gates that error-correcting codes implement easily are not universal on their own. Magic-state cultivation~\cite{cultivation} is one of the most economical ways known to make them. It prepares a T state in a small color code and checks it repeatedly with physical T gates, keeping the state only if every check passes. Simulations predict that cultivation reaches logical error rates that previously required distillation, at a cost comparable to that of a logical CNOT~\cite{cultivation}, and it has been demonstrated on hardware~\cite{googlecultivation}. Variants cultivate on surface codes~\cite{chenrp2,sahay} and prepare multi-qubit magic states~\cite{mincs,minmulti}, and earlier schemes also inject a state and verify it by postselection~\cite{li2015,chamberlandcross,chamberlandnoh,itogawa}.

These circuits have been designed with simulations that contain no T gates. Clifford circuits can be simulated efficiently and general circuits with T gates cannot, so cultivation circuits are simulated with every T gate replaced by an S gate, its Clifford square. We call this the S proxy. The proxy keeps the structure of every check, and the circuits that are proposed and built are chosen with it. Exact simulators that keep the T gates have appeared only recently~\cite{soft,takada,merlin,magna,wanzapirain}, and they show that the proxy is not faithful: the distance-three and distance-five circuits of Ref.~\cite{cultivation} tolerate fewer faults than their proxies~\cite{chan,wanzapirain}. Simulators judge one circuit at a time. Three questions remain. Is there a criterion that decides fault tolerance with real T gates for every circuit and explains its loss? How much fault tolerance do current checks lose, and does the loss grow with the code distance? Can a check keep all of it?

We answer them in turn (Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}). First, the fault tolerance of any cultivation circuit is decided by one stabilizer code on its T gates, together with a linear map that carries faults to Pauli errors on those T gates. Because the checks of cultivation are deterministic, this criterion has a closed form that can be evaluated in polynomial time. It also shows that cultivation needs at least as many T gates as distillation needs noisy inputs, and exactly as many if it ends in an ideal decoder. Second, current checks lose about half their distance. With real T gates, the GHZ-fold checks in use today tolerate only $(\delta-1)/2$ faults on color codes of distance $\delta=3$, $5$ and $7$, while their proxies tolerate $\delta-1$ at $\delta=3$ and $5$ and at least four at $\delta=7$. A single mechanism is responsible. Third, checks that avoid this mechanism keep the full distance at $\delta=3$ and $5$, and at least six of seven at $\delta=7$. They fit on a square grid with nearest-neighbor gates, and they fail orders of magnitude less often than the checks in use today.

\section{Cultivation and fault distance}
A cultivation circuit injects a T state into a triangular color code and checks the logical state, possibly growing the code between checks, up to a final distance $\delta$. Every nontrivial logical operator of the final code acts on at least $\delta$ physical qubits. A check applies a T or T$^\dagger$ gate to every data qubit, measures the logical X operator and undoes these gates. The pattern of T and T$^\dagger$ gates is chosen so that the check measures the logical operator $TXT^\dagger$, of which $|T\rangle$ is an eigenstate. A correct state therefore passes every check, and a state that fails is discarded. At the end an ideal decoder measures all stabilizers, keeps the state only if none is violated, and unencodes the logical qubit. This last step stands in for the later stages of a real protocol, in which the code is grown and protected by error correction, as in the simulations of Ref.~\cite{cultivation}. Ref.~\cite{mehta} analyzes what these stages cost when the final postselection must use noisy syndrome data. Figure~\ref{fig:concept}(a) sketches such a circuit, and Fig.~\ref{fig:small}(a) shows on the Bloch sphere what a single check does to an error.

Every operation of the circuit is noisy. A fault is a Pauli error after a preparation or a gate, or a wrong measurement outcome, and each location fails independently with probability of order $p$. A fault configuration is \emph{harmless} if the circuit then rejects the state or outputs the correct one, and \emph{harmful} if it can accept a state that differs from the correct one. The \emph{fault distance} $d$ is the smallest number of faults that can be harmful, so that an accepted state is wrong with probability of order $p^d$. It cannot exceed the code distance $\delta$, because $\delta$ errors on the data just before the decoder can form a logical error that the decoder does not see (Sec.~\ref{sm:proofs}). A circuit with $d=\delta$ keeps its full distance.

\section{One code decides}
The obstacle to an exact analysis is that a Pauli fault before a T gate becomes a non-Pauli error after it. We therefore move the T gates out of the way. A T gate on a qubit can be replaced by a small Clifford circuit that consumes an ancilla prepared in $|T\rangle$: a CNOT from the qubit to the ancilla, followed by a projection of the ancilla onto $|0\rangle$~\cite{zhouleungchuang},
\begin{equation}
 \sqrt2\,\langle0|_a\,\mathrm{CX}_{q\to a}\,|\psi\rangle_q|T\rangle_a=T|\psi\rangle_q .
 \label{eq:gadget}
\end{equation}
Doing this for all $t$ T gates leaves a noisy Clifford circuit, the \emph{skeleton}, that acts on $t$ ideal T states. The skeleton can be followed exactly with the stabilizer formalism. Every fault can be pushed to its end, where it either contradicts a measurement outcome that is fixed in the skeleton, which we call a \emph{detector}, or acts as a Pauli error on the $t$ T states.
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Consider a cultivation circuit, ending in its ideal decoder, with $t$ T gates, without feedforward and with every measurement postselected, that prepares a target state $\tau$ when no fault occurs. Suppose that no Pauli operator other than the identity fixes $\tau$, as is the case for a T state. Then there are a stabilizer code $C$ on $t$ qubits and two linear maps, $D$ from fault configurations to detector patterns and $E$ from fault configurations to Pauli operators on $t$ qubits, such that a fault configuration $F$ is harmless if and only if $D(F)\neq0$ or
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_C\,E(F)\,|T\rangle^{\otimes t}\in\mathbb C\,\Pi_C|T\rangle^{\otimes t},
 \label{eq:collapse}
\end{equation}
where $\Pi_C$ is the projection onto $C$. The map $E$ is defined up to phases and stabilizers of $C$.
\end{theorem}
In words, a fault configuration is harmless exactly when it is detected or when the code $C$ still projects the perturbed T states onto the correct encoded state, or onto zero [Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}(b)]. The code $C$ is fixed by the circuit and does not depend on the faults, which only choose the Pauli operator $E(F)$ that perturbs its inputs. Equation~\eqref{eq:collapse} is the condition that decides magic-state distillation, where perfect Clifford circuits measure a code on noisy T states~\cite{distillationpaper}. Cultivation differs only through the maps $D$ and $E$, which reject faults or carry them from anywhere in the circuit onto the T states. The pair $(D,E)$ plays the role that the detector error model plays for Clifford circuits~\cite{stim}. Exact simulators instead follow each fault through the T gates~\cite{soft,takada,merlin,magna,wanzapirain}, and Refs.~\cite{surti,chan} evaluate the Clifford error that a fault leaves at the end of the circuit. Here a single code, fixed by the circuit, judges every fault. Protocols with feedforward and random choices reduce to the circuit of a single accepted run without loss of fault distance (Proposition~\ref{prop:branch}). The proofs are in Sec.~\ref{sm:proofs}.

\paragraph*{An exact test.}
Condition~\eqref{eq:collapse} involves the state $|T\rangle^{\otimes t}$, whose dimension grows exponentially with $t$. In cultivation it can be decided without writing this state down, because the checks are deterministic: a Z error on the T inputs either leaves the encoded state unchanged, flips the logical qubit, or is rejected with certainty. One expects this when every check measures an operator of which the target is an eigenstate, and we verify it for every circuit we study. An X error is different, because $X|T\rangle=(|T\rangle+iZ|T\rangle)/\sqrt2$ is a superposition of a correct and a Z-flipped T state. A Pauli error whose X part acts on $|a|$ inputs therefore spreads over $2^{|a|}$ Z patterns, and the code sorts each of them into the correct state, the wrong state or rejection. Adding up the amplitudes gives the following result, which is stated precisely and proved in Sec.~\ref{sm:gauss}.
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:gauss}
If the checks of the circuit are deterministic in this sense (Definition~\ref{def:dc}), then for every fault configuration $F$ with $D(F)=0$, whose error $E(F)$ has an X part on $|a|$ inputs, there are two numbers $g_0(F)$ and $g_1(F)$, each a sum $\sum_z i^{q(z)}$ over a quadratic form $q$ with values modulo four, such that $F$ is accepted with probability $2^{-|a|}(|g_0|^2+|g_1|^2)$ relative to the fault-free circuit and outputs a wrong state with probability $2^{-|a|}|g_1|^2$. In particular, $F$ is harmful if and only if $g_1(F)\neq0$, which is decided with $O(t^3)$ operations.
\end{theorem}
Such Gauss sums are evaluated by eliminating one variable at a time. Each step either multiplies the sum by a simple factor or shows that it vanishes. Every fault configuration of a cultivation circuit can thus be judged exactly, with real T gates, at the cost of linear algebra. We combine this with a stim detector error model of the skeleton, which lists all fault classes with their detector patterns~\cite{stim}, and with a search for sets of faults whose detector patterns cancel. Fault distances are then certified with the same search as for Clifford circuits. A second, independent implementation follows the skeleton symbolically and decides Eq.~\eqref{eq:collapse} with state vectors. The two agree fault by fault on the distance-three circuit of Ref.~\cite{cultivation} (Secs.~\ref{sm:compute} and~\ref{sm:algorithm}).

\paragraph*{The cost in T gates.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} also bounds the number of T gates. Z faults on the T gates are never detected, and they act on the code $C$ exactly like noise on the inputs of a distillation protocol. A circuit of fault distance $d$ therefore contains a distillation protocol of order $d$. Conversely, every such protocol can be turned into a cultivation circuit of at least the same fault distance that ends in an ideal decoder of the protocol's code, as in the simulations of Ref.~\cite{cultivation} (Sec.~\ref{sm:distill}).
\begin{theorem}\label{cor:exact}
Every cultivation circuit of fault distance $d$ for one T state contains at least as many T gates as the least number of noisy inputs of a distillation protocol of order $d$. This number of T gates suffices if the circuit ends in an ideal decoder for its output code, and it is at least $3d-4$ for odd $d$ and $3d-6$ for even $d$.
\end{theorem}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:branch}, the lower bound holds for every accepted run of an adaptive protocol as well. Since distillation of order two needs nine noisy T states~\cite{distillationpaper}, fault distance two needs exactly nine T gates, and a nine-T circuit attains it (Sec.~\ref{sm:t9}). The ideal decoder matters only for the converse. A noisy final stage is a Clifford circuit and adds no T gates, but the fault distance then also depends on how that stage is built. To our knowledge, the lower bounds are new for distillation as well, because the code of every order-$d$ protocol has distance at least $d$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:shadow}). With a linear-programming bound for stabilizer codes, they improve Ref.~\cite{distillationpaper} for one T state from $d=6$ on (Sec.~\ref{sm:distill}). Published circuits are far from these bounds. The distance-five circuit of Ref.~\cite{cultivation} uses $53$ T gates for fault distance three, which fifteen T gates already reach, although our fifteen-T circuit accepts only one fault-free run in $1024$ (Sec.~\ref{sm:t9}). At practical distances, cultivation is limited by its checks, not by its number of T gates.

\section{Real T gates halve the distance}
All the smallest harmful fault configurations that we found share one structure, which the following lemma describes, up to two close variants treated in Sec.~\ref{sm:proofs}. Consider a color code of distance $\delta$ and a set $\ell$ of $\delta$ data qubits that supports a logical X operator.
\begin{lemma}[Coherent completion]\label{lem:completion}
Suppose that undetected faults leave an X error on a nonempty proper subset $u$ of $\ell$ just before the last layer of T gates, that further faults put X on the rest of $\ell$ after this layer, and that the circuit only measures stabilizers between this layer and the ideal decoder. Then the decoder accepts with probability $2^{-|u|}$ relative to the fault-free circuit and outputs a state of fidelity $1/2$ with the target. With S gates in place of T gates, the same faults are detected.
\end{lemma}
The reason is that a T gate turns an X error into a superposition, $TXT^\dagger\propto(I-iZ)X/\sqrt2$ [Fig.~\ref{fig:small}(a)]. After the last T layer, the error on $u$ is a superposition of $X^u$ with every Z error inside $u$. The decoder rejects every component that contains a Z error, since such an error acts on fewer than $\delta$ qubits of a minimum-weight logical support, so that it is neither a stabilizer nor a logical operator. It keeps the one component without a Z error, and the remaining faults complete $X^u$ to a logical operator. An S gate instead turns X into Y, whose Z part the decoder always detects. The proof is in Sec.~\ref{sm:proofs}. It follows that a check keeps its full distance only if no $s$ undetected faults can put X on more than $s$, but fewer than $\delta$, qubits of such a logical support before the last T layer (Sec.~\ref{sm:mechanism}).

Current checks violate this condition. They measure the logical operator with a GHZ fold, a tree of CNOTs that collects the parity of the data onto one qubit, which is measured before the tree is undone. When the tree is undone, a single X fault on one of its qubits spreads to a whole branch of data qubits, which we call a hook [Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}(c)]. With S gates the decoder detects this spread. With T gates, further faults complete it coherently: at $\delta=3$ one hook and one data fault suffice (Fig.~\ref{fig:gsj}), and in general $(\delta-1)/2$ hooks that each reach two qubits of a logical support and one data fault. We certified this loss on the published circuits and on fold checks that we built at larger distance.
\begin{proposition}[computer-assisted]\label{prop:gsj}\label{prop:fold}
With real T gates in place of the S gates of their simulations, (i) the distance-three and distance-five cultivation circuits of Ref.~\cite{cultivation} have fault distance two and three, while their proxies have three and five, and (ii) GHZ-fold checks of the same kind on color codes of distance three, five and seven have fault distance two, three and four, for every fold tree we tried.
\end{proposition}
In every case the fault distance with real T gates is $(\delta+1)/2$, about half the code distance, and the gap grows with $\delta$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:fd}). These failures are partly suppressed, since by Lemma~\ref{lem:completion} they are accepted only with probability $2^{-|u|}$. Even so, the leading term of the logical error of the distance-three circuit of Ref.~\cite{cultivation} is $32p^2/75$, in agreement with the exact series of Ref.~\cite{wanzapirain}. Because this term is of order $p^2$ instead of $p^3$, the proxy underestimates the logical error by a factor that grows as $p$ decreases. The circuit run on hardware in Ref.~\cite{googlecultivation} also has fault distance two with real T gates, against three for its proxy (Sec.~\ref{sm:google}). Secs.~\ref{sm:gsj} and~\ref{sm:mechanism} give the details, including flags that partly repair the fold.

\section{Keeping the distance}
Lemma~\ref{lem:completion} points to the remedy: no single fault should put X on more than one data qubit before the last T layer. A Shor-style check~\cite{shor} has this property. It prepares a cat state $(|0\cdots0\rangle+|1\cdots1\rangle)/\sqrt2$ on fresh qubits, verifies it by measuring parities of its qubits before it touches the data, couples each cat qubit to one data qubit by a CNOT, and reads the outcome of the check from the parity of the cat qubits in the X basis. A fault in the preparation that spreads X over several cat qubits is caught by the verification, and every other fault reaches at most one data qubit.

Such a check needs no long-range gates. On a square grid we grow the cat along a path of qubits from its middle outward, one CNOT per step in each direction. A single X fault on a path qubit then spreads only towards the end of the path beyond it, and the parity of the two ends, measured through an ancilla next to both, catches every such spread [Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}(c)]. Each data qubit sits next to its own path qubit. After the coupling we undo the growth, so that the outcome of the check sits on one qubit and the other path qubits return to $|0\rangle$, where measuring them gives further detectors. At $\delta=3$ the path runs around a rectangle, which the ancilla closes. At $\delta=5$ the path folds into a U on three rows, and the middle row measures the parities across the U and along its diagonals, which together catch every spread caused by two faults (Fig.~\ref{fig:nn}).
\begin{proposition}[computer-assisted]\label{prop:vcat}\label{prop:nn}
With real T gates and all-to-all connectivity, checks with a verified cat keep the full fault distance five on the distance-five color code and have at least five on the distance-seven code, where the same checks without verification have three and four. With nearest-neighbor gates on a square grid, the ring check on the distance-three code ($17$ qubits) and the ladder check on the distance-five code ($46$ qubits) have fault distance three and five, and the ladder on the distance-seven code ($91$ qubits) has at least six.
\end{proposition}
These checks are also cheap. We compare them under SI1000 noise, a standard model of superconducting hardware in which two-qubit gates fail with probability $p$, measurements with $5p$ and single-qubit gates with $p/10$~\cite{si1000}. At $p=10^{-3}$ the ring accepts $88\%$ of runs and fails with probability $0.056\,p^3+30\,p^4\approx9\times10^{-11}$. The best distance-three GHZ fold that we built fails about $600$ times more often ($0.049\,p^2$), accepts $43\%$ of runs and needs more than twice the qubits. The ladder accepts $39\%$ of runs, against $2\%$ to $9\%$ for our folds and unverified cats of the same distance, mostly because it needs no stabilizer measurements between checks. Without undoing the growth it accepts $55\%$ of runs and fails with probability $4\times10^{-14}$. For comparison, the published distance-five protocol, which in addition prepares its state, reaches $2\times10^{-9}$ at $16\%$ acceptance. Table~\ref{tab:main} collects the fault distances and Sec.~\ref{sm:nn} the costs.

These checks start from an ideally encoded state, like those of Proposition~\ref{prop:gsj}(ii). At distance three we also built a complete nearest-neighbor protocol in which every operation is noisy. It keeps fault distance three, so preparation does not limit the fault distance there. Its cost lies in the stabilizer measurements around the checks, which our version performs slowly, and it beats the circuit of the experiment of Ref.~\cite{googlecultivation} only for $p\lesssim3\times10^{-4}$ (Sec.~\ref{sm:nn}).

\section{Outlook}
Cultivation has so far been judged by simulations that replace its T gates by S gates. Theorem~\ref{thm:main} replaces this substitution by an exact criterion, a single stabilizer code on the T gates, and Theorem~\ref{thm:gauss} makes the criterion fast enough to certify realistic circuits. With it we find that the checks in use today keep only about half their distance, for the reason that Lemma~\ref{lem:completion} identifies, and that checks built to avoid it keep all of it at distances three and five, and at least six of seven at distance seven, with nearest-neighbor gates and a far lower logical error. Our results assume stochastic Pauli noise, as the simulations of cultivation do. Coherent errors and leakage, which experiments also see~\cite{googlecultivation} and which can raise the cost of cultivation~\cite{hines}, are outside this model. The main open problems are a cheap way to measure stabilizers on the grid around these checks, a certificate that the ladder keeps the full distance seven, and lower bounds on the gates that real-T checks need (Sec.~\ref{sm:open}).

\section{The criterion}\label{sm:proofs}
This section states the setting precisely and proves Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, Lemma~\ref{lem:completion} and the bound of the fault distance by the code distance. We first fix the circuits, the faults and the decoders that the main text describes informally.
\paragraph*{Circuits and protocols.}
Throughout, $|T\rangle=(|0\rangle+\omega|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$ with $\omega=e^{i\pi/4}$. We begin with the most general protocols we consider. A \emph{Clifford+T protocol} acts on qubits initialized in $|0\rangle$. It may use Clifford gates, T and T$^\dagger$ gates, Pauli measurements, classical randomness, feedforward of any operation on earlier outcomes, postselection and discarding, and it outputs $r$ qubits. Its target is a pure state $\tau$ with stabilizer nullity $\nu(\tau)=r$. This means that $\tau$ is fixed by no Pauli operator other than the identity, as is the case for $|T\rangle$ and $|\CCZ\rangle$. The circuits of the main text belong to a simpler class. A protocol is \emph{non-adaptive and fully postselected} if it is a fixed sequence of Clifford gates, T and T$^\dagger$ gates, projections $(I+sQ)/2$ with a Pauli $Q$ and a fixed sign $s$, and removals $\langle k|_q$ of qubits with fixed $k\in\{0,1\}$. We count T$^\dagger$ gates as T gates. Such a circuit has a single Kraus operator $K$, the linear map that it applies to its input. We call the circuit \emph{correct} if $K|0\rangle$ is a nonzero multiple of $|\tau\rangle$.

\paragraph*{Faults.}
Next we fix the noise model. Every preparation, gate and measurement is a \emph{location}, except those declared ideal. Removals are ideal. A fault at a location is either a Pauli operator on its support or the flip of a projection's sign. A Pauli fault acts immediately after a preparation or gate, or immediately before a projection. Faults at different locations compose. The \emph{fault space} $\fs$ is the $\F$-vector space of all assignments of Pauli operators and flips to locations. The weight $\wt(F)$ of $F\in\fs$ is the least number of single faults that compose to $F$. It is the number of locations at which $F$ acts, except that a Pauli fault and a flip at the same measurement count twice. Each location suffers each of its faults independently with probability $p\lambda>0$, with a constant $\lambda$ for each kind of fault. A run of an adaptive protocol is therefore described by its random choices and observed outcomes $b$, together with the faults $F$ at the locations executed along $b$, and it has a Kraus operator $K_{b,F}$. The pair $(b,F)$ is \emph{harmless} if $K_{b,F}|0\rangle\in\mathbb C|\tau\rangle$, which includes rejection. The output error after postselection is $\epsilon=O(p^d)$ exactly when every run with $\wt(F)<d$ is harmless and some fault-free run is accepted. The largest such $d$ is the \emph{fault distance}. It depends only on which faults are possible, not on their probabilities, as long as these are of order $p$. It is therefore the same, for example, under depolarizing noise, which applies one random Pauli operator per location. We call the harmful fault configurations of the least weight $d$ \emph{malignant}. Together they give the leading term of the output error.

\paragraph*{Ideal decoders.}
The output of cultivation is encoded, so we must also say how an encoded output is judged. A protocol whose output is encoded is judged after an ideal decoder. This decoder consists of projections onto the stabilizers of an $[[m,r,\delta]]$ code $Q$ on $m$ output qubits, followed by an ideal Clifford unencoding. We call the decoder \emph{admissible} if the fault-free state at its input lies in $Q$, so that it accepts the fault-free run with certainty. Its projections and the unencoding are ideal operations of the circuit, so the results below apply to the circuit together with its decoder. Simulations of cultivation use decoders of this kind~\cite{cultivation}. The admissibility condition is needed. Without it, the decoder could carry out the T-dependent checks itself. For example, nine noisy T states handed to an ideal decoder of the code of Proposition~\ref{prop:t9} below would already tolerate every single fault.

\paragraph*{Reduction to one run.}
A protocol may branch on its random choices and outcomes. For the fault distance it suffices to study one accepted run, as the following proposition shows.
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:branch}
Let a Clifford+T protocol have fault distance $d$, and let $b$ be any accepted fault-free run. The non-adaptive, fully postselected circuit formed by the operations executed along $b$, with every measurement replaced by the projection onto its outcome in $b$ and every discarded qubit removed with an outcome of nonzero amplitude, is correct and has fault distance at least $d$.
\end{proposition}
The proof below uses only one fact: a sum of positive operators is a multiple of a pure state exactly when each term is. As a consequence, every lower bound on the qubits, gates or T gates of correct non-adaptive circuits of fault distance $d$ holds for every accepted fault-free run of every protocol of fault distance $d$.

\paragraph*{The skeleton.}
In this notation, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} states in addition that $C$ has dimension $2^r$ with $\nu(\tau)=r$, that $\Pi_C|T\rangle^{\otimes t}\neq0$, that the fault-free skeleton acts as $c\,V\Pi_C$ with a Clifford isometry $V$ that maps $\Pi_C|T\rangle^{\otimes t}$ to a multiple of $|\tau\rangle$, and that a Z fault immediately before or after the $j$th T gate has $D=0$ and $E=Z_j$. The proof is given below. The pair $(D,E)$ plays the role of the detector error model of a Clifford circuit~\cite{stim,delfossepaetznick,bombinzx}, with the logical observables replaced by the Pauli group on the T gates and the test on them by the collapse condition~\eqref{eq:collapse}. Both maps are computed in polynomial time by following symbolically the Choi state of the skeleton, the state that it produces from one half of $t$ Bell pairs. Only the collapse condition involves the T states (Sec.~\ref{sm:compute}).

\paragraph*{Detectors and checks.}
A measurement whose outcome is fixed in the circuit need not be a detector of the skeleton, and this difference matters for T gates. The entries of $D$ belong to the projections whose outcome is fixed in the skeleton. Some projections have an outcome that is fixed in the circuit but depends on the T inputs in the skeleton. A check after a layer of T gates is an example. Such a projection is not a detector in this sense. It is part of the code $C$, and it rejects a fault only through Eq.~\eqref{eq:collapse}. That is, it removes only the components of $E(F)|T\rangle^{\otimes t}$ that leave the code. In the distance-three circuit of Ref.~\cite{cultivation}, 4 of the 20 projections are of this kind: the X measurement that reads out the logical check, and three X-type stabilizers measured after the final T layer. Wan and Zapirain likewise find that 16 of its 20 detectors remain Pauli webs with T gates~\cite{wanzapirain}. With S gates the circuit is Clifford, and all 20 are detectors.

\paragraph*{Related descriptions.}
Several earlier works describe how faults act in cultivation circuits, and we relate our description to them. Replacing T gates by gadgets is standard in simulation~\cite{bravyigosset}. For cultivation, Surti \emph{et al.} observed that every Pauli fault reaches the end of the circuit as a Clifford error~\cite{surti}. Chan \emph{et al.} computed the acceptance and fidelity of a code state under such an error~\cite{chan}. Exact simulators follow faults through the T gates~\cite{soft,clifft,symft,wanzhong,takada,wanzapirain,syqma}. Theorem~\ref{thm:main} instead moves the non-Clifford part to the inputs. There one code, independent of the fault, judges a Pauli operator that depends linearly on the fault.

\paragraph*{X errors on the inputs.}
The new feature relative to distillation is that $E(F)$ may have an X part. On one qubit, $X|T\rangle=(|T\rangle+iZ|T\rangle)/\sqrt2$ and $XZ|T\rangle=-(i|T\rangle+Z|T\rangle)/\sqrt2$. A Pauli operator $E=X^uZ^v$ therefore turns $|T\rangle^{\otimes t}$ into a superposition of the states $Z^{v+w}|T\rangle^{\otimes t}$ with $w$ supported on $u$, with amplitudes of modulus $2^{-|u|/2}$. To compare such operators with Z patterns, we use a weight that ignores phases and stabilizers. For a Pauli operator $E$ on the T inputs, let $|E|_S$ be the least number of qubits on which an element of $E\langle iI,S_C\rangle$ acts.
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:shadow}
(i)~If every pattern $v+w$ with $\operatorname{supp}w\subseteq\operatorname{supp}u$ satisfies the distillation condition $\Pi_CZ^{v+w}|T\rangle^{\otimes t}\in\mathbb C\,\Pi_C|T\rangle^{\otimes t}$, then $X^uZ^v$ satisfies Eq.~\eqref{eq:collapse}, and every $F$ with $E(F)=X^uZ^v$ is harmless. (ii)~If every Z pattern of weight below $d$ satisfies the distillation condition, then every Pauli operator $E$ with $|E|_S<d$ satisfies Eq.~\eqref{eq:collapse}, and $C$ has distance at least $d$.
\end{lemma}
Part~(i) says that an X error on the inputs is harmless when each of its Z components is. The smallest instance of~(i) is a single check (Fig.~\ref{fig:small}(a)). A projection onto $|T\rangle$ rejects $Z|T\rangle$, and it returns $X|T\rangle$ and $Y|T\rangle$ to $|T\rangle$ half of the time. X and Y faults before the check are therefore harmless, although they are neither detected nor stabilizers (Sec.~\ref{sm:example}). The converse of~(i) can fail, because harmful components may cancel. For the code on two qubits with the single stabilizer $X_1X_2$, the error $E=X_1X_2$ is a stabilizer and therefore harmless, while the Z patterns $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ in its expansion are harmful. Part~(ii) is where cultivation and distillation meet. A code that tolerates Z faults on fewer than $d$ inputs tolerates every Pauli fault on them. Since no Pauli operator fixes the target, such a code also has no logical operator of weight below $d$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:branch}]
We compare the accepted output of the whole protocol with the contribution of a single run. The unnormalized accepted output of the protocol is $\sum_{b',F'}q_{b',F'}\,K_{b',F'}\rho_0K_{b',F'}^\dagger$. The sum runs over accepted runs $b'$ and the fault configurations $F'$ along them, with probabilities $q_{b',F'}>0$. This expression is exact for stochastic Pauli noise, because faults are sampled classically at the locations actually executed. A run $(b,F)$ is harmless exactly when its own term is a multiple of $|\tau\rangle\langle\tau|$. Now fix an accepted fault-free run $b$. Along $b$, every random choice and every measurement outcome is fixed, so the operations executed along $b$ form a non-adaptive circuit. For every fault configuration $F$ at its locations, $K_{b,F}$ is the Kraus operator of this circuit with $F$ inserted, postselected on the outcomes of $b$. A discarded qubit contributes a partial trace, $\sum_k\langle k|\cdot|k\rangle$. If the sum is a multiple of a pure state, each term is. Replacing the partial trace by the removal $\langle k|$, for an outcome $k$ with $\langle k|K_{b,\emptyset}|0\rangle\neq0$, therefore keeps the circuit correct and every harmless configuration harmless. Hence every configuration with $\wt(F)<d$ is harmless in the circuit. The removals are ideal and add no locations.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
The proof has five steps. We build the skeleton, show that the signs of its projections act as Pauli operators, move the faults onto the T inputs, derive the collapse condition, and finally treat Z faults at T gates.

\emph{Skeleton.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:gadget} follows from the state $\mathrm{CX}_{q\to a}(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle)(|0\rangle+\omega|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$, whose component with $a$ in $|0\rangle$ is $(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta\omega|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$. We introduce one ancilla $a_j$ per T gate, prepared ideally in $|T\rangle$, and write T$^\dagger=S^\dagger T$ with an ideal $S^\dagger$. A fault of the $j$th T gate acts after the gadget. The circuit becomes $K_F=2^{t/2}K_{S,F}(|0\rangle\otimes|T\rangle^{\otimes t})$. Here the skeleton $K_{S,F}$ consists of Clifford gates, projections and removals, and $F$ is inserted at the same qubits and times. Let $M_F=K_{S,F}(|0\rangle\otimes\cdot)$, a linear map from $t$ qubits to $r$ qubits, and let $M=M_0$. As in the proof of Theorem~1 of Ref.~\cite{distillationpaper}, $M$ applied to half of $t$ Bell pairs gives a stabilizer state on $t+r$ qubits. By the local-Clifford normal form of bipartite stabilizer states, $M=c\,V\Pi_C$ with a stabilizer code $C$ on $t$ qubits and a Clifford isometry $V$ from $C$ onto its range. The range contains $|\tau\rangle$ and is a stabilizer subspace. Since no Pauli operator other than the identity fixes $\tau$, the range is the whole output space. So $\dim C=2^r$, and $V$ is unitary from $C$ onto $(\mathbb C^2)^{\otimes r}$.

\emph{Signs.} We treat each removal $\langle k|_q$ as a projection of $Z_q$ with sign $(-1)^k$ followed by an ideal removal. Let $\sigma\in\F^\mu$ list the signs of all $\mu$ projections relative to the circuit, so that $M^{(0)}=M$. Let $|\Psi^{(\sigma)}\rangle=(M^{(\sigma)}\otimes I)\sum_x|x\rangle|x\rangle$ be the unnormalized Choi vector. If we follow the circuit with the stabilizer formalism~\cite{aaronsongottesman}, the Choi vector stays a pure stabilizer state. The Pauli of every projection therefore either anticommutes with a current stabilizer or is one up to sign. In the first case the projection halves the squared norm and replaces that stabilizer by the projected Pauli with the chosen sign. In the second case it keeps the state or annihilates it, depending on the sign. The unsigned stabilizer groups and the norms of the nonzero $|\Psi^{(\sigma)}\rangle$ are therefore independent of $\sigma$. The signs required by the deterministic projections are affine functions of the earlier signs, so $|\Psi^{(\sigma)}\rangle\neq0$ exactly when $D_0\sigma=0$ for a linear map $D_0$. Two stabilizer states with the same unsigned group and norm differ by a Pauli operator and a phase. This Pauli operator is determined by the sign difference modulo the stabilizer group, and it depends on it linearly. Hence, for $D_0\sigma=0$, $|\Psi^{(\sigma)}\rangle=\zeta_\sigma(A_\sigma\otimes B_\sigma)|\Psi^{(0)}\rangle$ with $|\zeta_\sigma|=1$ and Pauli operators $A_\sigma$ on the output and $B_\sigma$ on the reference. In other words, $M^{(\sigma)}=\zeta_\sigma A_\sigma MB_\sigma^{\mathsf T}$.

\emph{Faults.} We move a Pauli $P$ inserted after an operation to the end of the circuit. A Clifford gate $G$ turns it into $GPG^\dagger$. A projection $(I+sQ)$ with which it anticommutes becomes $(I-sQ)$ as $P$ passes it. A removal absorbs the Z part of $P$ on the removed qubit, and its projection turns the X part into a sign flip. A sign flip changes $\sigma$ directly. Hence $M_F=\zeta\,P_{\mathrm{out}}(F)\,M^{(s(F))}$ with a phase $\zeta$, a Pauli $P_{\mathrm{out}}(F)$ on the output and a sign vector $s(F)$, both linear in $F$. We set $D(F)=D_0s(F)$, where $D_0$ has one row per projection, nonzero only for the projections whose outcome is fixed in the skeleton. If $D(F)\neq0$, then $M_F=0$ and the configuration is rejected. Otherwise $M_F=\zeta'P_{\mathrm{out}}A_{s}MB_{s}^{\mathsf T}$. It remains to move the output Pauli onto the inputs. For an output Pauli $R$, write $V\Pi_C=W(I\otimes\langle0|^{\otimes(t-r)})U\Pi_C$, with a Clifford unitary $U$ on $t$ qubits that maps $C$ onto $(\mathbb C^2)^{\otimes r}\otimes|0\rangle^{\otimes(t-r)}$ and a Clifford unitary $W$ on $r$ qubits. Then $RM=ML$ with the Pauli $L=U^\dagger(W^\dagger RW\otimes I)U$, which commutes with the stabilizers of $C$. Hence $M_F=\zeta''M\,E(F)$ with $E(F)=L(P_{\mathrm{out}}A_s)B_s^{\mathsf T}$. Finally, $ME\propto ME'$ holds exactly when $E'E^\dagger$ acts on $C$ as a scalar, that is, when it lies in $\langle iI,S_C\rangle$. This equivalence holds because $V$ is injective on $C$, and because a Pauli that anticommutes with a stabilizer maps $C$ to an orthogonal code. So $E$ is well defined and linear modulo $\langle iI,S_C\rangle$ on the configurations with $D(F)=0$, and we extend it linearly to all of $\fs$.

\emph{Collapse.} With these maps, $K_F|0\rangle=2^{t/2}\zeta''c\,V\Pi_CE(F)|T\rangle^{\otimes t}$ and $K_0|0\rangle\propto V\Pi_C|T\rangle^{\otimes t}\propto|\tau\rangle$. Since $V$ is injective on $C$, $K_F|0\rangle\in\mathbb C|\tau\rangle$ exactly when Eq.~\eqref{eq:collapse} holds.

\emph{Z faults at T gates.} A Z fault after the gadget of the $j$th T gate on qubit $q$ satisfies $Z_q\langle0|_a\mathrm{CX}_{q\to a}=\langle0|_aZ_aZ_q\mathrm{CX}_{q\to a}=\langle0|_a\mathrm{CX}_{q\to a}Z_a$. Here we used $\langle0|_a=\langle0|_aZ_a$ and $\mathrm{CX}_{q\to a}Z_a=Z_qZ_a\mathrm{CX}_{q\to a}$. The fault thus acts as $Z$ on the ideal input $a_j$, so $D=0$ and $E=Z_j$. A Z fault before the gate gives the same result, because Z commutes with T and with $S^\dagger$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:shadow}]
Part~(i) expands a coherent error into its Z components, and part~(ii) reduces a light Pauli operator to part~(i).
(i)~On one qubit, $X|T\rangle=(|T\rangle+iZ|T\rangle)/\sqrt2$, $XZ|T\rangle=-(i|T\rangle+Z|T\rangle)/\sqrt2$ and $Y=iXZ$. Up to a phase, therefore, $X^uZ^v|T\rangle^{\otimes t}=\sum_w\gamma_wZ^{v+w}|T\rangle^{\otimes t}$, summed over $w$ with $\operatorname{supp}w\subseteq\operatorname{supp}u$ and with $|\gamma_w|=2^{-|u|/2}$. If each projected term lies on the line $\mathbb C\,\Pi_C|T\rangle^{\otimes t}$, so does their sum, and Theorem~\ref{thm:main} applies. (ii)~Multiplying $E$ by an element of $\langle iI,S_C\rangle$ changes $\Pi_CE|T\rangle^{\otimes t}$ only by a phase. We may therefore take $E=X^uZ^v$ acting on fewer than $d$ qubits. Every pattern $v+w$ in~(i) is supported on these qubits, so it has weight below $d$, and~(i) applies. For the distance, let $E$ commute with $S_C$ and have $|E|_S<d$. Then $E$ commutes with $\Pi_C$, and $E\Pi_C|T\rangle^{\otimes t}=\Pi_CE|T\rangle^{\otimes t}$ is a multiple of $\Pi_C|T\rangle^{\otimes t}$. The multiple is nonzero because $E$ is unitary. Through the isometry $V$, $E$ acts on $C$ as a Pauli operator $P$ on $r$ qubits up to a phase, and $|\tau\rangle$ is an eigenvector of $P$. A Hermitian multiple $P'$ of $P$ has eigenvalues $\pm1$, so $P'$ or $-P'$ fixes $|\tau\rangle$. Since $\nu(\tau)=r$, $P'=\pm I$. Hence $E$ acts on $C$ as a scalar, that is, $E\in\langle iI,S_C\rangle$. Every logical operator outside $\langle iI,S_C\rangle$ therefore acts on at least $d$ qubits.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:completion}]
We expand the error after the last T layer into Pauli components and follow each component through the decoder. For $U_q=T^{\pm1}$, $U_qXU_q^\dagger=\bar\omega^{\pm1}S^{\pm1}X$ and $S^{\pm1}=\omega^{\pm1}(I\mp iZ)/\sqrt2$. Up to a phase, the error immediately after the layer is therefore $\prod_{q\in u}(I\mp iZ_q)X^u/2^{|u|/2}=2^{-|u|/2}\sum_{w\subseteq u}c_wZ^wX^u$ with $c_w=\prod_{q\in w}(\mp i)$. The faults after the layer turn each component into $X^{\ell\setminus u}Z^wX^u=\pm Z^wX^\ell$. The stabilizer measurements and the ideal decoder project onto the reference syndrome. The operator $X^\ell$ is a logical operator, so it commutes with all stabilizers. The operator $Z^w$ with $w\subseteq u\subsetneq\ell$ has $|w|<\delta$, so it is not a nontrivial logical operator. It therefore commutes with all X stabilizers only if it is a Z stabilizer. No Z stabilizer is supported inside $\ell$: since the color code is self-dual, the X stabilizer on the same support would multiply $X^\ell$ to a lighter logical operator. Hence $w=\emptyset$. The projection maps every other component to an orthogonal syndrome space and removes it. The accepted component is $2^{-|u|/2}X^\ell$ applied to the fault-free state. It has relative probability $2^{-|u|}$, and it decodes to $X_L|\tau\rangle$. For $|\tau\rangle=|T\rangle$, $|\langle T|X|T\rangle|^2=1/2$, so the output has fidelity $1/2$. In the proxy, $SXS^\dagger=Y$ up to a phase, so the error after the layer is the Pauli $Z^uX^\ell$ up to a phase. The operator $Z^u$ with $\emptyset\neq u\subsetneq\ell$ is neither a stabilizer nor a logical operator. It has a nontrivial syndrome, and the configuration is rejected.
\end{proof}
The same expansion settles the two neighboring cases, which also occur among the malignant configurations of Sec.~\ref{sm:gsj}. First, if the completing faults put Y instead of X on one qubit $q$ of $\ell\setminus u$, the accepted component must contain $Z^{u}Z_q$, which has a trivial syndrome only if $u\cup\{q\}=\ell$. For $|u|=\delta-1$ the configuration is therefore accepted with probability $2^{-|u|}$ and outputs $Y_L|\tau\rangle$ up to a phase, again of fidelity $1/2$, and for smaller $u$ it is rejected. Second, let the faults leave $X^\ell Z^y$ with $y\subseteq\ell$ before the last T layer, so that X covers all of $\ell$. Then exactly the two components with $w=y$ and $w=\ell\setminus y$ survive, and the configuration is accepted with probability $2^{1-\delta}$. The decoded output is proportional to $X_L(I+cZ_L)|\tau\rangle$, where $c=\pm i$ because $\delta$ is odd, and $c$ changes sign with the parity of $|y|$, since $c_{\ell\setminus y}/c_y=(-1)^{|y|}c_\ell$. For one sign the output is $|T\rangle$ and for the other it is orthogonal to $|T\rangle$. If the check is correct, $y=\emptyset$ is harmless: on the $[[7,1,3]]$ code, for every pattern of T and T$^\dagger$ gates whose check fixes the encoded T state, $UX^\ell U^\dagger$ acts on the code as the check operator for every minimum-weight support $\ell$. A Y error on one qubit of $\ell$ together with X on the rest therefore gives an output orthogonal to $|T\rangle$. We confirmed the lemma and both cases with state vectors on the $[[7,1,3]]$ code.

\paragraph*{The code distance bounds the fault distance.}
Finally, the fault distance cannot exceed the distance of the code of an ideal decoder.
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:singleton}
If a correct circuit ends with an admissible ideal decoder for an $[[m,r,\delta]]$ code, and each of the $m$ qubits has an idle location immediately before the decoder, then its fault distance $d$ is at most $\delta$, and $m\geq r+2(d-1)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A logical Pauli of weight $\delta$ placed at the idle locations commutes with the stabilizers and passes the decoder. It then applies a nontrivial Pauli to the decoded state, and the result differs from $|\tau\rangle$ because $\nu(\tau)=r$. The inequality is the quantum Singleton bound~\cite{knilllaflamme}.
\end{proof}

\section{Deterministic checks and Gauss sums}\label{sm:gauss}
This section proves Theorem~\ref{thm:gauss} of the main text in a more precise form. Lemma~\ref{lem:shadow}(i) is only a sufficient condition for harmlessness. When the checks are deterministic, in the following sense, the collapse condition can be decided exactly in polynomial time.
\begin{definition}\label{def:dc}
A correct circuit for $|T\rangle$ has \emph{deterministic checks} if there are a subspace $\Sigma\subseteq\F^t$ and $\ell\in\F^t$ such that $Z^e\in\pm S_C$ for $e\in\Sigma$, $Z^\ell$ commutes with $S_C$ and $VZ^\ell V^\dagger$ acts on the output as $Z$, and $\Pi_CZ^e|T\rangle^{\otimes t}=0$ for every $e\notin\Sigma\cup(\ell+\Sigma)$.
\end{definition}
Here $\Sigma$ collects the Z patterns that are stabilizers of $C$ up to sign, and $Z^\ell$ is the logical Z. Every Z error on the T gates is then a stabilizer of $C$, the logical Z, or rejected with certainty. This is what one expects when every check measures an operator of which the target is an eigenstate, but it is not automatic, since Clifford gates can give a Z error an X part before it meets another T gate. We therefore test the definition on every circuit, with the transform of Sec.~\ref{sm:algorithm}, and all circuits we study pass. It is not the case for code-based distillation, where Z errors on the inputs are detected only with some probability. For $e\in\Sigma$ we write $Z^e=\xi(e)g_e$ with $g_e\in S_C$. These signs define a character $\xi$ of $\Sigma$, that is, a homomorphism to $\{\pm1\}$, which we extend to a character $\chi$ of $\F^t$.
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:gaussfull}
For a circuit with deterministic checks and a configuration with $D(F)=0$ and $E(F)=X^aZ^b$,
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_CE(F)|T\rangle^{\otimes t}=\mu\,2^{-|a|/2}\bigl(g_0+g_1Z^\ell\bigr)\Pi_C|T\rangle^{\otimes t},
 \label{eq:out}
\end{equation}
with $|\mu|=1$ and, for $c\in\{0,1\}$,
\begin{equation}
 g_c=\sum_{w\leq a,\;w+b\in c\ell+\Sigma}i^{|w|}\,\chi(w+b+c\ell).
 \label{eq:g}
\end{equation}
The configuration is accepted with probability $2^{-|a|}(|g_0|^2+|g_1|^2)$ relative to the fault-free run, its wrong output has weight $2^{-|a|}|g_1|^2$, and it is harmful if and only if $g_1\neq0$.
\end{theorem}
In Eq.~\eqref{eq:g}, $w\leq a$ means that the support of $w$ lies inside the support of $a$. We call the weight $2^{-|a|}|g_1|^2$ of the wrong output the bad weight. Theorem~\ref{thm:gauss} of the main text restates this theorem, with $a$ the X part of $E(F)$ and with Proposition~\ref{prop:poly} below for the cost.

Definition~\ref{def:dc} removes every Z pattern outside the two cosets $\Sigma$ and $\ell+\Sigma$, and the two surviving terms are orthogonal and of equal norm because $\langle T|Z|T\rangle=0$. Each $g_c$ is an exponential sum $\sum_{z\in\F^m}i^{q(z)}$ of a $\Z_4$-valued quadratic form, as the proof of the following proposition shows.
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:poly}
Each $g_c$ is computed with $O(t^3)$ operations, and $|g_c|$ is zero or a power of $\sqrt2$. In particular the acceptance and the bad weight of every fault configuration are dyadic rationals.
\end{proposition}
The coherence of real T gates enters only through these sums. Judging the terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:g} one at a time, and calling a configuration harmful when one of them lies in $\ell+\Sigma$, would give fault distance one for every circuit we studied: a single X fault before a check has such a term, which the check removes only through the cancellation $g_1=0$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:gaussfull}]
We expand $X^aZ^b|T\rangle^{\otimes t}$ in Z patterns and apply Definition~\ref{def:dc} to each term. On one qubit, $|T\rangle=(|0\rangle+\omega|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$, $Z|T\rangle=(|0\rangle-\omega|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$ and $X|T\rangle=\omega(|0\rangle+\bar\omega|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$. Writing $|0\rangle+\bar\omega|1\rangle=\alpha(|0\rangle+\omega|1\rangle)+\beta(|0\rangle-\omega|1\rangle)$ gives $\alpha+\beta=1$ and $\alpha-\beta=\bar\omega^2=-i$. Hence $\alpha=\bar\omega/\sqrt2$, $\beta=i\bar\omega/\sqrt2$ and $X|T\rangle=(|T\rangle+iZ|T\rangle)/\sqrt2$. Taking the tensor product over $\mathrm{supp}\,a$ and using $X^aZ^b=(-1)^{|a\wedge b|}Z^bX^a$, we obtain
\begin{equation}
 X^aZ^b|T\rangle^{\otimes t}=(-1)^{|a\wedge b|}\,2^{-|a|/2}\sum_{w\leq a}i^{|w|}Z^{b+w}|T\rangle^{\otimes t}.
\end{equation}
Now apply $\Pi_C$. By Definition~\ref{def:dc}, the terms with $b+w\notin\Sigma\cup(\ell+\Sigma)$ vanish. If $e\in c\ell+\Sigma$, then $e+c\ell\in\Sigma$, $Z^{e+c\ell}=\xi(e+c\ell)g$ with $g\in S_C$, and $\Pi_Cg=\Pi_C$. Since $Z^\ell$ commutes with $S_C$, it commutes with $\Pi_C$, so $\Pi_CZ^e|T\rangle^{\otimes t}=\chi(e+c\ell)Z^{c\ell}\Pi_C|T\rangle^{\otimes t}$. This proves Eq.~\eqref{eq:out} with $\mu=(-1)^{|a\wedge b|}$. For the probabilities, note that the vector $Z^\ell\Pi_C|T\rangle^{\otimes t}$ has the same norm as $\Pi_C|T\rangle^{\otimes t}$. The isometry $V$ maps the two to multiples, of equal modulus, of $Z|T\rangle$ and $|T\rangle$, which are orthogonal. The acceptance and the bad weight follow, and the bad weight is positive exactly when $g_1\neq0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:poly}]
We first write $g_c$ as a sum over a quadratic form and then remove its variables one at a time. The set $\{w\leq a: w+b\in c\ell+\Sigma\}$ is empty or an affine subspace $w_0+\langle k_1,\dots,k_m\rangle$ of $\F^{|a|}$, which Gaussian elimination finds in $O(t^3)$. For binary vectors $v_0,\dots,v_m$, inclusion and exclusion give $|v_0+\dots+v_m|=\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}(-2)^{|S|-1}|\bigwedge_{s\in S}v_s|$, and the terms with $|S|\geq3$ vanish modulo four. We write $\chi(w)=(-1)^{x\cdot w}$. Since $2(x\cdot w)\bmod4$ is linear in $z$, the summand of Eq.~\eqref{eq:g} is $i^{q(z)}$ with
\begin{equation}
 q(z)=q_0+\sum_lL_lz_l+2\sum_{l<l'}B_{ll'}z_lz_{l'},\quad L_l=|k_l|-2|w_0\wedge k_l|+2\,x\cdot k_l,\quad B_{ll'}=|k_l\wedge k_{l'}|\bmod2 .
\end{equation}
To sum out $z_v$, let $N$ be the set of its neighbors, those with $B_{vl}=1$, and let $y=\bigoplus_{l\in N}z_l$. The sum over $z_v$ is $1+i^{L_v}(-1)^y$. If $L_v$ is odd, this is $(1+i)\,i^{3y}$ or $(1-i)\,i^{y}$. The parity $y$, read as an integer modulo four, is $\sum_{l\in N}z_l-2\sum_{l<l'}z_lz_{l'}$, so $q$ stays quadratic. If $L_v$ is even, the sum over $z_v$ is $2[y=L_v/2]$, where the bracket is one when the condition holds and zero otherwise. If $N$ is empty, the sum then vanishes or is multiplied by two. Otherwise the constraint expresses one variable of $N$ as a parity of the others, which is substituted into $q$ in the same way. Each step costs $O(m^2)$ and multiplies the modulus by $\sqrt2$ or $2$, or returns zero. Hence $|g_c|$ is zero or a power of $\sqrt2$, and the acceptance $2^{-|a|}(|g_0|^2+|g_1|^2)$ and the bad weight $2^{-|a|}|g_1|^2$ are dyadic. We checked the procedure against direct summation on 400 random forms with up to eight variables. An independent implementation (\texttt{scripts/cultivation\_gauss\_check.py}) agrees with direct summation on 2000 further forms, and it confirms Theorem~\ref{thm:gaussfull} against state vectors for every Pauli operator on the skeleton codes of single-qubit circuits with one, two and three checks ($t=3$, $5$ and $7$).
\end{proof}

\section{Cultivation as distillation}\label{sm:distill}
This section proves Theorem~\ref{cor:exact}. Its first half says that a circuit of fault distance $d$ needs as many T gates as an order-$d$ distillation protocol needs inputs, and it follows directly from Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. Its second half, that this number of T gates also suffices, needs a construction.
\begin{corollary}\label{cor:tcount}
In a noise model in which every T gate may suffer a Z fault, every accepted run of a protocol of fault distance $d$ executes at least $n_{\min}(\tau,d)$ T gates, the least number of noisy T inputs of any order-$d$ distillation protocol for $\tau$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
We show that the code on the T gates defines a distillation protocol. By Proposition~\ref{prop:branch} it suffices to consider a correct non-adaptive circuit with $t$ T gates and fault distance $d$. Configurations of Z faults at T gates have $D=0$ and $E=Z^e$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, each of them with $|e|<d$ therefore satisfies $\Pi_CZ^e|T\rangle^{\otimes t}\in\mathbb C\,\Pi_C|T\rangle^{\otimes t}$. Measuring the stabilizers of $C$ on $t$ noisy T states, postselecting and decoding with $V$ is therefore an order-$d$ distillation protocol~\cite{distillationpaper}.
\end{proof}
The proof uses only the \emph{order} $d_C$ of the code. This is the least weight of a Z pattern that violates the distillation condition, and it is the suppression order of the distillation protocol that measures $C$ on noisy T states. The Clifford part limits the fault distance in a second way, by spreading faults. We call $d_{\mathrm{sp}}=\min\{\wt(F):D(F)=0,\ |E(F)|_S>\wt(F)\}$ the \emph{spreading distance} of the circuit. It is the least number of undetected faults whose Pauli operator on the T inputs is heavier than their number.
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:attain}
A correct non-adaptive circuit with code $C$ has fault distance $d$ with $\min(d_C,d_{\mathrm{sp}})\leq d\leq d_C$. Conversely, for every stabilizer code $C$ on $t$ qubits that encodes a target $\tau$ with $\nu(\tau)=r$ as $\Pi_C|T\rangle^{\otimes t}\neq0$, there is a correct non-adaptive circuit with $t$ T gates and an admissible ideal decoder whose code on the T gates is $C$ and whose fault distance is $d_C$.
\end{theorem}
The upper bound is the proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:tcount}, and the lower bound is Lemma~\ref{lem:shadow}(ii). For the converse, proved below, the circuit applies T to $t$ qubits in $|{+}\rangle$, so that its T inputs are its data. It then measures the generators of $C$ one after another with cat states~\cite{shor}, each checked by $d_C-1$ rounds of parity measurements. A cat state that passes a fault-free round has no X error, so $k$ faults in one measurement leave a data error of weight at most $k$. The output stays encoded in $C$. The ideal decoder, which measures $C$ again, rejects every run whose recorded signs disagree with the data error. Hence $d_{\mathrm{sp}}\geq d_C$.

Two limits of Theorem~\ref{thm:attain} should be kept in mind. First, the converse relies on the ideal decoder. It re-measures every generator, so it catches every wrong sign of the last noisy measurement and every data error left after it. Each of its projections repeats an earlier measurement with only commuting operations in between, so it is a detector of the skeleton and makes no check that depends on the T inputs, unlike the decoder of the published circuit (Sec.~\ref{sm:gsj}). This is allowed by the definition of an admissible decoder and is the convention of Ref.~\cite{cultivation}, but a real final stage must do this work with noisy operations. The output is also left in the code $C$ itself, for example the $15$-qubit Reed-Muller code, rather than in a code that is later grown into a surface code. The cost of that transfer is outside our model. The lower bound on the number of T gates, Corollary~\ref{cor:tcount}, has no such caveat, since an ideal decoder can only help a protocol. Second, the lower bound $\min(d_C,d_{\mathrm{sp}})$ does not make the fault distance cheap to compute. Finding $d_{\mathrm{sp}}$ still means enumerating undetected configurations up to weight $d_C$, and it only removes the collapse tests. For the published circuit of Proposition~\ref{prop:gsj} it leaves $d\in\{2,3\}$ (Sec.~\ref{sm:codes}). The value of the theorem is structural. It splits the fault distance into the order of the code and the spreading of faults, and it shows that every code is attained.

The equality combines Corollary~\ref{cor:tcount} with Theorem~\ref{thm:attain} for the code of an optimal distillation protocol, which has distance at least $d$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:shadow}(ii). The last bounds follow from Rains' theorem that a quantum code of length $n$ corrects at most $\lfloor(n+1)/6\rfloor$ errors~\cite{rains}. The exact values of Ref.~\cite{distillationpaper} thus hold for cultivation: fault distance two needs nine T gates for one T state and eight for $|\CCZ\rangle$. The distance bound is a statement about distillation alone. By Lemma~\ref{lem:shadow}(ii), the code of every order-$d$ distillation protocol for $\tau$ has distance at least $d$, so $n_{\min}(\tau,d)$ is at least the least length of such a code. We present it here as a new distillation result. It improves Table~I of Ref.~\cite{distillationpaper}, for one T state from $d=6$ on and for $|\CCZ\rangle$ from $d=4$ on, and from $d=7$ on it also improves the analytic bound $2d$ for one T state proved there. For codes with transversal T gates, distance and suppression order are known to be linked~\cite{bravyihaah}, and Kalra and Prakash bound suppression exponents as functions of the length for self-orthogonal codes over GF(4)~\cite{kalraprakash}. We did not find the statement for arbitrary postselected protocols. The linear-programming bound for stabilizer codes with Rains' shadow enumerator~\cite{shorlaflamme,rains}, certified in exact arithmetic (Table~\ref{tab:lp}), raises these lower bounds. For one T state at $d=6,\dots,12$ it raises them from $\max(2d,d+7)$ to $16$, $17$, $22$, $23$, $28$, $29$ and $34$. For $|\CCZ\rangle$ at $d=4,\dots,8$ it raises them from $11$, $12$, $13$, $15$ and $17$ to $12$, $14$, $17$, $20$ and $23$.

\begin{lemma}[Checked cat-state measurement]\label{lem:cat}
Let a Pauli operator $g$ act on $w$ data qubits with letters $P_1,\dots,P_w$. Prepare $(|0\rangle^{\otimes w}+|1\rangle^{\otimes w})/\sqrt2$ on cat qubits $c_1,\dots,c_w$ by preparing $c_1$ in $|{+}\rangle$ and the others in $|0\rangle$ and applying CNOTs from $c_1$. In each of $\theta$ rounds, measure $Z_{c_1}Z_{c_i}$ for $i=2,\dots,w$ with a fresh ancilla in $|0\rangle$, the target of CNOTs from $c_1$ and $c_i$, and keep the outcome $+1$. Apply controlled-$P_i$ from $c_i$ to the $i$th data qubit for every $i$. Measure every $c_i$ in the X basis and keep the runs whose outcomes have product $s$. If at most $\theta$ faults occur in this gadget and the run is kept, it acts on the data as $R\,(I+s'g)/2$ up to a nonzero scalar, with $s'=\pm s$ and a Pauli operator $R$ for which $R$ or $Rg$ acts on at most as many qubits as there are faults.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We track the X errors that the cat qubits carry onto the data. Without faults, X outcomes with product $(-1)^{|x|}$ leave a nonzero multiple of $[I+(-1)^{|x|}g]/2$ on the data. Now push every fault to the end of the gadget. Z errors on cat qubits, which Z errors on the ancillas also produce, and flips of the X measurements change the product of the X outcomes. X errors on the ancillas and flips of their measurements change only the checks. A Pauli on a data qubit before its controlled Pauli can be moved after it, at the cost of a Z error on its cat qubit when it anticommutes with $P_i$. Let $x\in\F^w$ mark the X errors on the cat qubits at the controlled Paulis. They reach the data as $P^x=\bigotimes_iP_i^{x_i}$, and $P^{x+\mathbf1}=\pm gP^x$. During the rounds the cat qubits are only controls of CNOTs, so a fault there changes at most one entry of $x$. The same holds for a fault on a cat qubit before its own controlled Pauli. A fault in a controlled Pauli or on a data qubit adds a Pauli on one data qubit, since an X error on $c_i$ after its controlled Pauli no longer spreads. If the preparation of the cat state is fault-free, $x$ starts at $\mathbf0$. Otherwise at most $\theta-1$ faults fall into the $\theta$ rounds, so one round is fault-free. That round reads $x_1+x_i$ correctly for every $i$, so a kept run has $x\in\{\mathbf0,\mathbf1\}$ after it, and only later faults change $x$. In both cases the data error is $R$ or $\pm Rg$, with $R$ acting on at most as many qubits as there are faults.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:attain}]
We first prove the two bounds and then build the circuit of the converse.

\emph{Bounds.} Choose a pattern $e$ of weight $d_C$ that violates the distillation condition. Z faults at the corresponding T gates have $D=0$ and $E=Z^e$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:main}). They are therefore harmful, and $d\leq d_C$. If $\wt(F)<\min(d_C,d_{\mathrm{sp}})$ and $D(F)=0$, then $|E(F)|_S\leq\wt(F)<d_C$, and $F$ is harmless by Lemma~\ref{lem:shadow}(ii).

\emph{Construction.} Let $g_1,\dots,g_m$ generate $S_C$, with signs $s_k$. The circuit prepares $t$ data qubits in $|{+}\rangle$ and applies T to each. It then measures $g_1,\dots,g_m$ in turn with the gadget of Lemma~\ref{lem:cat}, with $\theta=d_C-1$ and $s=s_k$. Finally it passes the data to the ideal decoder of $C$, which projects onto $(I+s_kg_k)/2$ for every $k$ and unencodes. The circuit keeps every run whose cat-state outcomes have the required product. Fixing one such outcome string for each cat state gives the same Kraus operator up to a scalar, so the construction is a fully postselected circuit in the sense of Sec.~\ref{sm:proofs}. Every projection of the decoder onto $g_k$ follows the measurement of $g_k$ with only commuting operations in between, so it is fixed in the skeleton and is a detector. Without faults the checks of the cat states are deterministic and the measurements apply $\Pi_C$. The output is then a nonzero multiple of $\Pi_C|T\rangle^{\otimes t}$, so the circuit is correct and the decoder is admissible. In the skeleton, the gadget of a T gate on $|{+}\rangle$ maps its input to the data qubit identically, $\sqrt2\langle0|_a\mathrm{CX}_{q\to a}|{+}\rangle_q|\psi\rangle_a=|\psi\rangle_q$, so the code of the circuit is $C$. Now let $F$ have fewer than $d_C$ faults, and let every check of the cat states pass. By Lemma~\ref{lem:cat}, the $k$th measurement acts as $R_k(I+s'_kg_k)/2$, with $R_k$ or $R_kg_k$ acting on at most as many qubits as there are faults in that gadget. Every other fault, on a T gate or an idle data qubit, is a Pauli operator on one data qubit. So is a fault on the preparation of $|{+}\rangle$, which leaves $|{+}\rangle$ or $|{-}\rangle$, with $T|{-}\rangle=ZT|{+}\rangle$. We move all these Pauli operators to the T inputs. A Pauli $R$ moved past $(I+s'g)/2$ turns it into $(I+(-1)^{\langle R,g\rangle}s'g)/2$, where $\langle R,g\rangle$ is zero if $R$ and $g$ commute and one otherwise. The circuit therefore applies $\prod_k(I+s_kg_k)/2\cdot\prod_k(I+s''_kg_k)/2\cdot R\,|T\rangle^{\otimes t}$ with signs $s''_k$, where $R$ is the product of all the Paulis. Since the projections commute, this vanishes unless $s''_k=s_k$ for every $k$, and it is then a multiple of $\Pi_CR|T\rangle^{\otimes t}$. Replacing $R_k$ by $R_kg_k$ changes $R$ only within $R\langle iI,S_C\rangle$, so $|R|_S\leq\wt(F)<d_C$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:shadow}(ii) shows that $F$ is harmless. Faults that fail a check are rejected. Hence the fault distance is at least $d_C$, and by the bound above it equals $d_C$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{cor:exact}]
We prove the equality first and the two lower bounds after it. By Corollary~\ref{cor:tcount}, every accepted run executes at least $n_{\min}(\tau,d)$ T gates. Conversely, by Theorem~1 of Ref.~\cite{distillationpaper}, an order-$d$ distillation protocol on $n=n_{\min}(\tau,d)$ inputs yields a stabilizer code $C$ on $n$ qubits whose projection of $|T\rangle^{\otimes n}$ encodes $\tau$ and satisfies the distillation condition for all patterns of weight below $d$. So $d_C\geq d$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:attain} turns $C$ into a circuit with $n$ T gates and fault distance $d_C\geq d$. For the lower bounds, Lemma~\ref{lem:shadow}(ii) shows that $C$, and the code of every order-$d$ distillation protocol, has $r$ logical qubits and distance at least $d$. Such a code corrects $\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor$ errors. By Ref.~\cite{rains} its length is therefore at least $6\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor-1$, which is $3d-4$ for odd $d$. For even $d$, feeding one input of an order-$d$ protocol with $I/2$ gives an order-$(d-1)$ protocol~\cite{distillationpaper}, so $n_{\min}(\tau,d)\geq n_{\min}(\tau,d-1)+1\geq3d-6$.
\end{proof}

\section{Codes on the T gates}\label{sm:codes}
This section collects results about the code $C$ on the T gates. We bound the length of the codes that a given fault distance needs, test the construction of Theorem~\ref{thm:attain} on three codes, and look at the code of a published circuit.

\paragraph*{Linear-programming bound.}
By Theorem~\ref{cor:exact}, lower bounds on the length of stabilizer codes are lower bounds on the number of T gates. Here we explain how we obtain the bounds of Table~\ref{tab:lp}. Consider an $[[n,r]]$ stabilizer code with stabilizer group $S$. Let $A_j$ be the number of elements of weight $j$ of $S$, and $B_j$ the number of elements of weight $j$ of its normalizer, both counted up to phases. Let $S_j$ be the number of Pauli operators of weight $j$ in the shadow of the code, the set of Pauli operators that commute with the elements of even weight of $S$ and anticommute with those of odd weight. These numbers satisfy~\cite{shorlaflamme,rains}
\begin{align*}
&A_0=1,\qquad\textstyle\sum_jA_j=2^{n-r},\qquad B_j=2^{r-n}\sum_iK_j(i)A_i,\qquad S_j=2^{r-n}\sum_i(-1)^iK_j(i)A_i,\\
&B_j\geq A_j\geq0,\qquad S_j\geq0\qquad(0\leq j\leq n),
\end{align*}
where $K_j(i)=\sum_s(-1)^s3^{j-s}\binom is\binom{n-i}{j-s}$ is the coefficient of $y^j$ in $(1+3y)^{n-i}(1-y)^i$. A code of distance $d$ also satisfies $B_j=A_j$ for $1\leq j<d$. If no real $A$ satisfies this system, there is no $[[n,r,d]]$ stabilizer code. There is then none of smaller length either, since appending a qubit in $|0\rangle$, with stabilizer Z, keeps the distance. For each entry of Table~\ref{tab:lp}, the script \texttt{scripts/cultivation\_codes.py} (option \texttt{--lp}) shows that the system has no solution at length $n-1$. It computes a Farkas certificate of infeasibility, a combination of the constraints that leads to a contradiction, by an exact phase-one simplex method in rational arithmetic, and checks the certificate in integer arithmetic. As a test, the weight distributions of the nine-input, eight-input and $15$-input codes satisfy the system. For $r=1$ and odd $d$ the resulting bounds coincide with Rains' bound $3d-4$.

\paragraph*{The construction.}
We tested the construction of Theorem~\ref{thm:attain} on three codes. The same script builds the circuit for each code, treats every operation as a noisy location, and computes the fault map. Pairs of faults are enumerated through their detector vectors. Since $D$ is linear, a pair is undetected exactly when its two faults have the same $D$. The first two codes are the eight-input CCZ code, with $8$ T gates, $17$ qubits, $97$ two-qubit gates and one round of checks, and the nine-input T code, with $9$ T gates, $16$ qubits, $128$ two-qubit gates and one round. None of the $1860$ single faults of the first circuit and none of the $2450$ single faults of the second is harmful. Both circuits therefore have fault distance two, the order of their codes. The third code is the $15$-to-$1$ code. With two rounds of checks ($15$ T gates, $24$ qubits, $458$ two-qubit gates), none of the $8612$ single faults and none of the $37{,}028{,}662$ pairs is harmful. With one round ($310$ two-qubit gates), none of the $5874$ single faults and none of the $17{,}214{,}903$ pairs is harmful. Without faults, the three circuits accept with the probabilities $1/16$, $1/128$ and $1/1024$ with which their codes accept ideal T states, and every projection of their decoders is a detector. The script also checks Lemma~\ref{lem:shadow}(ii) for these codes: all Pauli operators that act on fewer than $d_C$ inputs collapse, $27$ of them for the nine-input code, $24$ for the eight-input code and $990$ for the $15$-to-$1$ code.

\paragraph*{The published circuit.}
The code of a published circuit shows that a heavy Pauli operator on the T inputs is needed for harm but does not cause it by itself. We take the circuit of Proposition~\ref{prop:gsj} with $15$ T gates, the distance-three circuit of Ref.~\cite{cultivation}. All Z patterns of weights one and two on its T gates collapse, and $35$ of weight three do not, so $d_C=3$. Undetected single faults have $|E|_S\leq1$. Among the undetected pairs of faults at distinct locations, $10{,}942$, $12{,}338$, $3887$, $60$ and $301$ have $|E|_S=0,\dots,4$, and all of these are harmless. Another $87$ have $|E|_S=5$, and $48$ of them are harmful. A Pauli of weight at least $d_C$ modulo $S_C$ is thus necessary for harm but not sufficient.

\section{The smallest case}\label{sm:t9}
By Theorem~\ref{cor:exact}, fault distance two for one T state needs exactly nine T gates. This section gives a small circuit that attains this bound, proves its fault distance, and describes the integer program that found it and its logical error under depolarizing noise. The construction of Theorem~\ref{thm:attain} with the nine-input factory code of Ref.~\cite{distillationpaper} also uses nine T gates, but it needs $16$ qubits and $128$ two-qubit gates. An integer program over the fault map finds a smaller circuit.
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:t9}
There is a circuit with nine T gates, eleven qubits and $34$ two-qubit gates, ending in an admissible ideal decoder, that prepares one T state with fault distance two.
\end{proposition}
The circuit (Fig.~\ref{fig:small}(b)) prepares nine qubits in $|{+}\rangle$ and applies T to each, so that its T inputs are its data qubits. It then measures eight independent stabilizers of weight four of the nine-input factory code of Ref.~\cite{distillationpaper}, each with one ancilla and four controlled-Pauli gates. It keeps only the outcomes of the factory, which occur with probability $1/128$ without faults. The factory code has distance three. The output stays encoded in it, and the decoder, which measures the stabilizers again, is admissible.

The argument for fault distance two sorts the faults by where they occur. Faults on the preparations and T gates are single-qubit Paulis on the inputs. They collapse because the code distills to order two, by Lemma~\ref{lem:shadow}(ii). A fault during the measurement of the $k$th stabilizer leaves a Pauli $R$ on the data and may flip the outcome. It is detected if $R$ anticommutes with a stabilizer measured before, or if the flip does not match the commutation of $R$ with the $k$th stabilizer. Otherwise it acts as $R$ on the inputs, and every Pauli of weight two on the inputs modulo the stabilizers is harmful (see the integer program below). The weight-two errors that the measurement circuits spread to the data must therefore anticommute with a stabilizer measured earlier, or be caught by a flag~\cite{chaoreichardt}, an extra qubit that detects errors of the ancilla that would spread to two data qubits. The choice of the eight stabilizers, their order, the order of the qubits in each measurement and the flags is an integer program over the fault map, described below. The first measurement needs a flag, since no stabilizer precedes it. Gurobi shows in under a minute that one flag suffices. The proof is exact: the remaining single faults reduce to Paulis on the inputs of weight at most one modulo the stabilizers. An enumeration of all $627$ single faults confirms it, both with the fault map and by direct simulation (Fig.~\ref{fig:small}(c)).

The construction is a distillation at the physical level, in the spirit of Ref.~\cite{itogawa}, made fault tolerant by the order of its measurements. Idle faults do not lower its fault distance, since an idle fault equals a fault after the preceding operation on the same qubit. The circuit minimizes T gates, not cost. It accepts one fault-free run in $128$. Under the uniform depolarizing noise of Ref.~\cite{cultivation}, its logical error per accepted run is $53p^2$ to leading order without idle noise, and $1.9\times10^3p^2$ with idle noise in an as-soon-as-possible schedule (see below). For comparison, the distance-three circuit of Ref.~\cite{cultivation} has the same fault distance with $15$ T gates, $15$ qubits and $81$ CNOTs. It accepts every fault-free run and has error $32p^2/75$.

For fault distance three, Theorem~\ref{thm:attain} applied to the $15$-to-$1$ code of Bravyi and Kitaev~\cite{bravyikitaev} gives a circuit with $15$ T gates, $24$ qubits and $310$ two-qubit gates (Sec.~\ref{sm:codes}). With the lower bound of Ref.~\cite{distillationpaper}, the least number of T gates for fault distance three is therefore between ten and fifteen. Like the nine-T circuit, this circuit is built to use few T gates, not to be cheap. Without faults it accepts one run in $1024$, because the outcomes of its stabilizer measurements are random and only one outcome string is kept. The distance-five circuit of Ref.~\cite{cultivation} reaches the same fault distance with real T gates using $53$ T gates, $42$ qubits and $477$ two-qubit gates, and it accepts every fault-free run.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:t9}]
Let $g_1,\dots,g_8$ be the measured stabilizers in their order (Table~\ref{tab:t9}). They generate the stabilizer group $S$ of the factory code, which is the code $C$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} for this circuit, with the T gates numbered by their qubits. A fault after a preparation or a T gate is, up to stabilizers of $|{+}\rangle$, a Pauli of weight one on the T inputs, with no flips. Its $Z$ part collapses, by the exact certificate of the single-fault minors in Ref.~\cite{distillationpaper}, and so does its $X$ part, by Lemma~\ref{lem:shadow}(i). Consider a fault in the measurement of $g_k$, including its ancilla and flag. Pushed to the end of the measurement circuit, it becomes a Pauli $R$ on the data, a flip $\phi$ of the outcome of $g_k$ and a flip $\psi$ of the flag. If $\psi=1$, the flag detects it. If $R$ anticommutes with some $g_j$, $j<k$, the decoder detects it, because $g_j$ was projected before $R$ and is projected again after it, with only commuting projections in between. If $\phi$ differs from the commutator $\langle R,g_k\rangle$, the decoder detects it, because the projection onto $g_k$ then has the wrong sign relative to the decoder's. Otherwise $R$ commutes with $g_1,\dots,g_{k-1}$ and, together with the flip, equals $R$ applied before the measurement of $g_k$. It can then be moved to the inputs, and the fault is harmless exactly when $R$ collapses. An exact computation over $\F$ (\texttt{scripts/cultivation\_t9.py}) sorts the $573$ faults in the measurement circuits of Table~\ref{tab:t9}: $37$ raise the flag, $288$ leave an $R$ that anticommutes with an earlier stabilizer, $152$ flip the outcome inconsistently with $R$, and the remaining $96$ leave an $R$ of weight at most one modulo $S$, which collapses, since multiplication by a stabilizer changes $\Pi_CR|T\rangle^{\otimes9}$ only by a sign. Measurement flips and faults on the decoder's input are covered by the same cases.
\end{proof}

\paragraph*{Integer program.}
The circuit was found by an integer program that chooses the stabilizers, their order, the order of the gates in each measurement and the flags. Let $\mathcal W$ be the $18$ elements of weight four of the stabilizer group $S$ of the factory code, with their signs, which span $S$. Let $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ be the symplectic form, and let $\mathcal C$ be the $48$ minimal linearly dependent subsets of $\mathcal W$. For $w\in\mathcal W$, an order $\pi$ of its support and $f\in\{0,1\}$, the measurement circuit $(w,\pi,f)$ is that of Table~\ref{tab:t9}, with a flag if $f=1$. Let $\mathcal R(w,\pi,f)$ be the set of data Paulis $R$ of the single faults in this circuit that raise no flag, satisfy $\phi=\langle R,w\rangle$ and do not collapse. By the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:t9}, these are the faults that are harmless only if $R$ anticommutes with a stabilizer measured earlier. They are computed once, by Pauli propagation and the collapse test on nine T states. Every Pauli that collapses has weight at most one modulo $S$. Binary variables $s_w$ select the measured stabilizers, $y_{vw}$ equals one if $v$ is measured before $w$, and $z_{w\pi f}$ selects the order and the flag of the measurement of $w$:
\begin{align*}
\min_{s,y,z}\quad&\sum_{w\in\mathcal W}\sum_{\pi}z_{w\pi1}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&\textstyle\sum_{w\in\mathcal W}s_w=8,\qquad \sum_{w\in C}s_w\leq|C|-1\quad(C\in\mathcal C),\\
&\textstyle\sum_{\pi,f}z_{w\pi f}=s_w\quad(w\in\mathcal W),\\
&y_{vw}\leq s_v,\quad y_{vw}\leq s_w,\quad y_{vw}+y_{wv}\leq1,\quad y_{vw}+y_{wv}\geq s_v+s_w-1\quad(v\neq w),\\
&y_{uv}+y_{vw}+y_{wu}\leq2\quad(u,v,w\text{ distinct}),\\
&\textstyle z_{w\pi f}\leq\sum_{v\in\mathcal W:\,\langle R,v\rangle=1}y_{vw}\quad(R\in\mathcal R(w,\pi,f)),\\
&s_w,\ y_{vw},\ z_{w\pi f}\in\{0,1\}.
\end{align*}
The objective counts the flags. The first line of constraints selects eight independent stabilizers, which then generate $S$, since a subset of $\mathcal W$ is independent exactly when it contains no set of $\mathcal C$. The second line picks one order and one flag setting for each selected stabilizer. The constraints on $y$ make it a linear order of the selected stabilizers, since a tournament without directed triangles is transitive. The constraint on $z$ and $\mathcal R$ requires every dangerous Pauli, that is, every element of $\mathcal R(w,\pi,f)$, to anticommute with a stabilizer measured before. The program has $1188$ variables and $4777$ constraints. Gurobi~10 finds a design with one flag in $46$\,s. This design is optimal, because the first measurement needs a flag. The script \texttt{scripts/cultivation\_t9.py} builds the program (option \texttt{--mip}), verifies the circuit, and writes \texttt{results/cultivation/t9\_circuit.json}. Of the $627$ single faults, $477$ are detected, $13$ rejected, $45$ trivial and $92$ absorbed (Fig.~\ref{fig:small}(c)). Of the $192{,}546$ pairs, $7146$ are harmful.

\paragraph*{Depolarizing noise.}
We also computed the logical error of the circuit under depolarizing noise. Under the uniform depolarizing model of Sec.~\ref{sm:gsj} without idle noise, $6279$ pairs of faults with nonzero probability are harmful. The logical error per accepted run is $c_2p^2+O(p^3)$ with $c_2=53.1$. Pairs of faults on the preparations and T gates, which act as faults of the factory's inputs, contribute $576$ of the harmful pairs. To include idle noise, we schedule every operation as early as possible, except that the ancilla and flag are prepared just before their first gate. The circuit then has $411$ idle locations, $409$ of them on the data. With idle noise, $132{,}968$ pairs are harmful and $c_2=1.9\times10^3$. Most of this comes from pairs of idle faults on the data, which wait while the eight stabilizers are measured one after another.

\section{Computing the fault map}\label{sm:compute}
We implement Theorems~\ref{thm:main} and~\ref{thm:gauss} twice, independently. This section describes the first implementation, the script \texttt{scripts/cultiv.py}. It computes the maps $D$ and $E$ symbolically and decides the collapse condition with state vectors, so it serves for small circuits. The second implementation, built on stim and Gauss sums, is the subject of Sec.~\ref{sm:algorithm}.

The script \texttt{scripts/cultiv.py} takes a non-adaptive circuit given as a list of operations: preparations, Clifford gates, T and T$^\dagger$ gates, projections (with or without reset of the measured qubit), discards of qubits in known eigenstates, and identity operations that carry idle noise. As in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, it follows the Choi state of the skeleton on the physical qubits, the gadget inputs $a_j$ and their references $r_j$. The stabilizer generators are stored as binary vectors. The sign of each generator is stored as a linear form in the flips $\sigma$ of all projections. This is all that the maps need, since constant signs only affect phases. A projection is handled in one of two ways. If its Pauli anticommutes with a generator, it replaces that generator, with the form $\sigma_k$ of the $k$th projection. If its Pauli is in the group, it adds the detector row $\sigma_k+\sum_i\ell_i$, where $\ell_i$ are the forms of the generators that multiply to it.

For a fault $F$, Pauli propagation through the skeleton gives the flips $s(F)$ and the output Pauli $P_{\mathrm{out}}(F)$. Then $D(F)$ is the value of the detector rows on $s(F)$. The generators whose forms evaluate to one on $s(F)$ change sign. The destabilizers, the Pauli operators that each anticommute with exactly one generator, convert these sign changes into a Pauli $G$ on the physical qubits and references. Multiplying $GP_{\mathrm{out}}$ by the stabilizer with the same physical part, found by elimination, leaves a Pauli on the references. This Pauli is $E(F)$. It is reduced modulo $S_C$ by a fixed echelon basis, so that the reduction is linear. When the circuit does not map onto its whole output space, as when the output is encoded and decoded, part $U(F)$ of the physical Pauli may remain unmatched. It is kept, and it vanishes whenever $D(F)=0$ for the circuits of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. The triple $(D,U,E)$ of a configuration is the sum of those of its faults.

For $D(F)=0$, harmlessness depends only on $(U,E)$, and one simulation decides it. A Pauli $E=X^uZ^v$ on the T inputs has the same effect as replacing T$^{k}$ by T$^{k-2}$ at the gates in $u$, which is what the gadget does with input $X|T\rangle$, and applying Z after the gates in $v$. The circuit so modified, followed by $U$, is simulated with state vectors, and its output is compared with the fault-free one. Pairs of faults are enumerated through the classes of their triples, so that each class is simulated once.

The script \texttt{scripts/cultiv\_test.py} tests this method against direct simulation of the faulty circuit. It uses $300$ random circuits of two or three qubits with up to three T gates and two projections, with all single faults and random pairs, $42{,}367$ configurations in all. Harmlessness from the fault map agrees with direct simulation on every one. For the $65$ circuits whose skeleton map has full row rank, the script also computes the skeleton map $M_F$ densely, by feeding basis states to the gadgets. It checks for $9{,}271$ configurations that $M_F=0$ exactly when $D(F)\neq0$, and that otherwise $U(F)=0$ and $M_F\propto ME(F)$.

A further script, \texttt{scripts/cultivation\_check.py}, tests Theorem~\ref{thm:main} with its own state-vector code. For all single faults and up to 800 pairs of faults, it compares the faulty output with physical T gates with the output of the skeleton fed with ideal T states, which tests the gadget identity. It then checks that the skeleton map is zero or a multiple of $ME$ for a Pauli $E$ on the T inputs, that harmlessness computed directly agrees with the collapse condition, that $E=Z_j$ for Z faults at T gates, and that $E(F_1+F_2)=E(F_1)E(F_2)$ modulo $\langle iI,S_C\rangle$. We ran it on circuits for $\CS|{+}{+}\rangle$ and $|\CCZ\rangle$ and on ten random circuits on two qubits with two to four T gates and one or two projections. The gadget identity holds for all 10{,}542 configurations tested. In the six circuits whose skeleton map has full row rank, as Theorem~\ref{thm:main} assumes, the other statements hold for all 5{,}316 configurations, all 24 T gates and all 3{,}726 pairs tested.

\section{Exact evaluation at scale}\label{sm:algorithm}
This section describes the second implementation, which handles large circuits. The circuits of Propositions~\ref{prop:gsj} and~\ref{prop:vcat} have up to $254$ qubits and $77$ T gates, beyond the reach of state vectors. We evaluate them with an independent implementation built on stim~\cite{stim}. In it, the fault map of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is a detector error model, and the collapse condition is decided by the Gauss sums of Theorem~\ref{thm:gauss}.

In outline, one stim detector error model of the skeleton lists every fault class with its probability, its detector pattern $D$ and its error $E$. One Walsh-Hadamard transform finds $\Sigma$ and $\ell$ and checks Definition~\ref{def:dc}, after which every evaluation is a Gauss sum. The fault distance follows from a meet-in-the-middle search on detector patterns, and the logical error from sampling the error model with the exact weight of each sample.

\paragraph*{Skeleton and Choi state.}
Every T gate on qubit $q$ becomes $\mathrm{CX}_{q,a_j}$ followed by a measurement of $a_j$ in the Z basis. A T$^\dagger$ gate becomes the same followed by $S^\dagger_q$. Here $a_j$ is half of a noiseless Bell pair with a reference $r_j$. Noise channels stay where they were, and the gadgets, Bell pairs and final decoding are noiseless. A noiseless tableau run fixes the reference outcomes. Every outcome that is random in the skeleton is fixed so that the circuit's own detectors read zero, and the remaining free outcomes are chosen. The state left on the references and the output qubit is the Choi state $|\Phi\rangle$ of $V\Pi_C$, with $t+1$ stabilizer generators $g_i$.

\paragraph*{Detectors and observables.}
The detectors are a basis of the parities of measurement records that are deterministic in the noiseless skeleton. We find them as the GF(2) null space of noiseless samples. The observables are the $g_i$, measured at the end, each completed by the record parity that makes it deterministic. One detector error model of the skeleton then gives every fault class with its probability $p_c$, detector pattern $D_c$ and observable flips $o_c$. For $D=0$, the effect of the faults on the Choi state is a Pauli $R$ on the references whose commutation pattern with the $g_i$ is $o$. A fixed right inverse over GF(2) gives $R$ from $o$, and $R=E(F)$ up to transposition.

\paragraph*{Exact evaluation.}
Row reduction of the $g_i$ writes the Choi state as a sum over an affine subspace, $|\Phi\rangle=\sum_{x\in x_0+\mathrm{span}(u_1,\dots,u_\kappa)}c(x)|x\rangle$ with $c(x_0)=1$ and $c(x+u_i)=i^{\varphi_i}(-1)^{z_i\cdot x}c(x)$. Here $x_{\mathrm{out}}$ and $x_{\mathrm{ref}}$ below denote the output and reference bits of $x$. For $R=X^aZ^b$ the output is
\begin{equation}
 v_y=\sum_{x:\,x_{\mathrm{out}}=y}c(x)(-1)^{b\cdot x}\omega^{|x_{\mathrm{ref}}\oplus a|},
\end{equation}
and the acceptance and bad weight of the fault class are $|v|^2/|v_0|^2$ and $|v|^2/|v_0|^2-|\langle v_0,v\rangle|^2/|v_0|^4$. A Z pattern $e$ acts through $\beta(e)=(e\cdot u_1,\dots,e\cdot u_\kappa)\in\F^\kappa$ and the sign $(-1)^{e\cdot x_0}$. One Walsh-Hadamard transform of length $2^\kappa$ over the coordinates therefore gives the output of every Z pattern. Definition~\ref{def:dc} holds when exactly two values of $\beta$ give a nonzero output, $0$ and $\beta(\ell)$. Then $\Sigma=\beta^{-1}(0)$ and $\chi(e)=(-1)^{e\cdot x_0}$, and the conditions $w+b\in c\ell+\Sigma$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:g} become the linear equations $\beta(w)=\beta(b)+c\beta(\ell)$. Every further class is evaluated with the Gauss sums of Proposition~\ref{prop:poly}. The direct sum above, of cost $2^\kappa$, serves as a check. The affine dimension is $\kappa=5$ for the $\delta=3$ circuit of Ref.~\cite{cultivation}, 15 for its $\delta=5$ circuit, 11 for the one-round cat checks at $\delta=5$, 21 for two rounds and 20 at $\delta=7$.

\paragraph*{Searches and rates.}
The fault distance is the least $k$ for which some $k$ fault classes have total detector pattern zero and a harmful total $o$. We call $k$ the weight of the search. Detector patterns are compressed by a random GF(2)-linear 64-bit hash. Weight three uses a sorted table of single hashes and checks every hit on the exact patterns. Weight four groups pairs of classes by hash and evaluates every distinct observable mask, the total $o$, of two pairs in the same group. A hash collision can only add masks. The certificates that find no harmful mask are therefore exact, and every harmful mask found is confirmed by a 4-set whose exact detector patterns cancel. Weight five splits each configuration canonically into its three smallest and two largest classes. The logical error at moderate $p$ comes from sampling the detector error model, with the exact acceptance and bad weight of each sampled class. For rare failures we use the decomposition $P_{\mathrm{bad}}=\prod_c(1-p_c)\sum_kA_k$ with $A_k=\sum_{|S|=k}\prod_{c\in S}r_c\,[D_S=0]\,\mathrm{bad}(o_S)$ and $r_c=p_c/(1-p_c)$. We evaluate $A_k$ by drawing $k-2$ classes with probability proportional to $\prod r_c$ and summing exactly over all completing pairs. On the $\delta=3$ circuit this agrees with the sampled logical error within statistical error.

\paragraph*{Validation.}
We validated the second implementation on the $\delta=3$ circuit of Ref.~\cite{cultivation} with real T gates. A state-vector simulation inserted each of the 2,444 single physical faults and 400 random pairs explicitly and postselected on the reference outcomes. It agrees with the algorithm in rejection for all 2,224 rejected configurations, and in acceptance and bad weight to $3\times10^{-16}$ for the 620 accepted ones. All 96 malignant pairs agree to the same precision. Each has acceptance $1/4$ and bad weight $1/8$ or $1/4$, according to whether its output has infidelity $1/2$ or $1$. These are the $96$ harmful pairs that the first implementation finds under the same uniform noise with idle errors (Sec.~\ref{sm:gsj}). They reduce to its $48$ pairs when only the operations of the circuit carry faults, with the same leading coefficient $32/75$. With S in place of T, the sampled acceptance and logical error at $p=3\times10^{-3}$ agree with direct stim sampling of the proxy circuit. The acceptance is 0.3251 in both, and the logical error is $1.08(13)\times10^{-5}$ against $1.02(12)\times10^{-5}$. The Gauss sums agree with the direct sum on random sums of one to four fault classes, all to $5\times10^{-16}$. We tested 335 classes of the $\delta=3$ double check, 380 of the $\delta=5$ protocol, 356 of the one-round verified cat at $\delta=5$ and 60 of the unverified cat at $\delta=7$. At $\kappa=20$ the direct sum takes 0.22~s per class and the Gauss sums 1~ms. Every circuit that we study has deterministic checks. On one workstation, certifying fault distance five for the one-round checks at distance five takes seconds, and the weight-four certificates at distance seven take minutes.

\section{The published circuits}\label{sm:gsj}
This section gives the details behind part (i) of Proposition~\ref{prop:gsj}, for the distance-three and distance-five circuits of Ref.~\cite{cultivation}. With T gates, the distance-three circuit has fault distance two~\cite{chan,wanzapirain}. Theorem~\ref{thm:main} recovers this and decides every class of faults exactly.

The distance-three circuit consists of a unitary injection followed by one double check. We compose it from the published chunks~\cite{gsjcode} ($15$ qubits, $15$ T gates, $81$ CNOTs) and place T gates where its stabilizer simulations use S gates. As in Ref.~\cite{cultivation}, we keep the final stabilizer measurements as an admissible ideal decoder, and we treat every operation as a noisy location. Then no single fault is harmful, and $48$ of the $966{,}391$ pairs are, while with S gates no pair is harmful. Fig.~\ref{fig:gsj}(a) shows one of the $48$ pairs. Its mechanism was identified in Ref.~\cite{chan}. The last layer of T gates turns an X error on two qubits into $[(X+Y)/\sqrt2]^{\otimes2}$. The final X-type checks act only through $C$. They remove the components with Z parts, but not $X\otimes X$. Direct simulation confirms every harmful pair. In the language of Theorem~\ref{thm:attain}, the loss comes from the spreading of faults, not from the code: the code on the $15$ T gates has order $d_C=3$, so a circuit with the same code reaches fault distance three, but the published circuit has spreading distance two (Sec.~\ref{sm:codes}).

We use the uniform depolarizing noise of Ref.~\cite{cultivation}, with idle noise in every time step of the chunks. Under this noise $96$ pairs are harmful. Write $\epsilon_T$ and $\epsilon_S$ for the logical error per accepted run with T and with S gates. Then $\epsilon_T=32p^2/75+O(p^3)$, while $\epsilon_S=O(p^3)$ (derived below). The coefficient equals the exact one of Wan and Zapirain~\cite{wanzapirain}, who contracted a tensor network for an independently compiled circuit. The state-vector simulations published with the circuit~\cite{gsjcode}, extrapolated linearly to $p=0$, give $(\epsilon_T-\epsilon_S)/p^2=0.45\pm0.08$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:gsj}(b)). At $p=10^{-3}$ the measured difference, $(5.8\pm0.6)\times10^{-7}$, is more than half of $\epsilon_T$, and at smaller $p$ it dominates.

\paragraph*{Reconstruction.}
We rebuilt the distance-three circuit from the published code. Its repository defines the circuit of Proposition~\ref{prop:gsj} as two stim chunks, the unitary injection with one round of stabilizer measurements and the distance-three double-cat check, whose boundary conditions are measurements of the color-code stabilizers and of the logical $Y$. In the chunks, S and S\_DAG stand for T and T$^\dagger$. We joined the two chunks (\texttt{data/gsj\_cultivation/}) by the coordinates of their qubits, dropped the boundary measurements between them, kept the final stabilizer measurements as an ideal decoder, and replaced S and S\_DAG by T and T$^\dagger$. Every measurement of the resulting circuit is deterministic, with T and with S, and the fault-free circuit accepts with probability one, so the decoder is admissible. With T gates, $4$ of the $20$ projections are not deterministic in the skeleton: the X measurement of the check and the three X-type stabilizer measurements of the decoder. They act through the code $C$, so the ideal decoder takes part in rejecting faults, as does any decoder that postselects on all stabilizers. Wan and Zapirain postselect in the same way, with a noiseless final projection onto every face stabilizer~\cite{wanzapirain}, and the agreement of the coefficient below suggests that the published state-vector simulations~\cite{gsjcode} do too. Exchanging T and T$^\dagger$ throughout gives the same $48$ harmful pairs. Grouped into fault classes, that is, single faults with the same detector pattern and the same Pauli operator on the T inputs, they form $9$ harmful pairs of classes, with and without idle noise (\texttt{scripts/cultivation\_gsj\_classes.py}). These are the nine that the exact evaluation of Sec.~\ref{sm:algorithm} finds. Chan \emph{et al.} group the error events of their own compilation by the signs they flip and the Pauli operator they leave at the end, and count the configurations whose output has fidelity zero. They find four of two faults~\cite{chan}. Grouped their way, our $48$ pairs form $12$ classes, four with fidelity zero, as in Ref.~\cite{chan}, and eight with fidelity one half (\texttt{scripts/cultivation\_codes.py}). The fault distance agrees with Ref.~\cite{chan}, and the leading coefficient with the independent exact result of Wan and Zapirain. The repository composes the chunks with its own compiler and re-layers the result. We did not run that code, so our idle noise follows the time steps of the chunk text.

\paragraph*{Noise.}
We now derive the coefficient $32/75$. With one location per operation, every Pauli error at every preparation, gate and measurement, and every flip of a measurement, is a fault. This gives $1397$ faults in all on $137$ operations. We use the uniform depolarizing model of Ref.~\cite{gsjcode} with strength $p$. It applies single-qubit depolarization after T (S) gates and two-qubit depolarization after CNOTs. It applies a bit flip after a $Z$ reset and a phase flip after an X reset, and it flips every measurement result. Finally it applies single-qubit depolarization to every qubit that is idle in a time step. We apply this idle noise only to qubits between preparation and measurement, since noise on other qubits is removed by the next reset. This gives $436$ locations, $299$ of them idle. The logical error per accepted run is $\epsilon=\sum_Fw_Fh_F/\sum_Fw_F\tr\Lambda(\rho_F)$. Here $w_F$ is the probability of the fault configuration $F$, and $h_F$ is the probability that run $F$ is accepted with a wrong output. The denominator is $1+O(p)$. The coefficient of $p^2$ in the numerator is a sum over the $96$ harmful pairs. These are $24$ pairs of two CNOT faults, $24$ of a CNOT and a T fault, $32$ of an idle and a CNOT fault, $8$ of an idle and a T fault, and $8$ of two idle faults. Each pair contributes the product of its probabilities divided by $p^2$, times its relative acceptance ($1/4$ for each) and the infidelity ($1/2$ or $1$) of its output. The sum equals $32/75$. This is the leading coefficient of the exact series of Wan and Zapirain~\cite{wanzapirain}, whose circuit was compiled independently and has $518$ fault locations. The coefficient therefore does not depend on our layering of the idle noise. The S version has no harmful pair.

\paragraph*{Flags at distance three.}
We now show that simple flags cannot repair the distance-three circuit. A flag on qubit $q$ over the window $[t_1,t_2)$ of operations of the check is an extra qubit prepared in $|0\rangle$. It is the target of a CNOT from $q$ before operation $t_1$ and of another before operation $t_2$, and it is measured in the Z basis, keeping only the outcome $+1$. It is a detector when no operation in the window changes the Z value of $q$, that is, when no CNOT targets $q$ and $q$ is neither measured nor reset. It then detects a fault $F$ exactly when the X part of the Pauli frame of $F$ on $q$ differs at $t_1$ and $t_2$. We write $\chi_K(F)\in\{0,1\}$ for this, for each of the $5590$ such candidates $K$. A pair $(F_1,F_2)$ is detected by $K$ when $\chi_K(F_1)\neq\chi_K(F_2)$. The fewest flags that detect every harmful pair therefore solve
\begin{align*}
\min_x\ \sum_Kx_K\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_{K:\,\chi_K(F_1)\neq\chi_K(F_2)}x_K\geq1\ \text{for every harmful pair }(F_1,F_2),\qquad x_K\in\{0,1\}.
\end{align*}
The program is infeasible. In two of the pairs the X errors arise on targets of CNOTs, and no candidate detects either fault. Covering the other $46$ pairs takes eight flags. Since Chan \emph{et al.} restore fault distance three with two flag qubits and four additional layers of CNOTs~\cite{chan}, flags outside this family suffice. This model ignores the faults that the flags themselves would add. The infeasibility does not depend on them. The script \texttt{scripts/cultivation\_gsj.py} carries out all computations of this section and writes \texttt{results/cultivation/gsj\_d3.json}.

\paragraph*{The distance-five circuit.}
The $\delta=5$ circuit of Ref.~\cite{cultivation} has two properties that only appear with real T gates. The first concerns the pattern of T and T$^\dagger$ gates in its checks. On the 19-qubit color code, the transversal check $\bigotimes_q U_qXU_q^\dagger$, with $U_q\in\{T,T^\dagger\}$, is logical only if every face $f$ contains $|f|/2\bmod2$ T$^\dagger$ gates~\cite{chan}. Among such patterns, the parity of the total number of T$^\dagger$ gates decides whether the check stabilizes the injected state $T^\dagger|{+}\rangle$ or its conjugate. With the wrong parity the check projects the state instead of detecting errors. Half of the runs are then lost, Definition~\ref{def:dc} fails and the circuit has fault distance two. The same holds at $\delta=3$, where transversal T acts on the $[[7,1,3]]$ code as the logical T$^\dagger$. The second property concerns the growth from $\delta=3$ to $\delta=5$. Its random outcomes leave X-type Pauli frames, and with real T gates these must be undone before the $\delta=5$ T layers, as in Ref.~\cite{soft}. We analyze the corresponding branch of maximal weight. With these choices, the full protocol has fault distance three, with leading term $1.5\,p^3$ under uniform noise (Table~\ref{tab:full}). This extends the analysis of the final double check in Refs.~\cite{chan,wanzapirain}. Its logical error agrees with the GPU simulation of Ref.~\cite{soft} (Table~\ref{tab:rates}).

\paragraph*{Malignant configurations.}
Here we describe the malignant configurations of the two published circuits. They involve hooks: X faults on a qubit of the fold tree that the unfold spreads to several data qubits (Sec.~\ref{sm:mechanism}). At $\delta=3$ there are nine malignant fault classes, of weight two. Each consists of an X or Y fault on a fold qubit and an X or Y fault on a data qubit at the final T layer. Immediately before that layer they leave X on two or three data qubits. At $\delta=5$ there are nine malignant classes, of weight three. Each consists of two hooks and one data fault, and they leave X on four or five data qubits. Their acceptance is $2^{-4}$. Their bad weight is $2^{-5}$ when the data fault completes X on four qubits, as Lemma~\ref{lem:completion} predicts, and $2^{-4}$ when X already covers a whole logical support before the layer, with Z on an odd number of its qubits, the second case discussed after the proof of the lemma.

\paragraph*{Flags at distance five.}
The lemma also limits what flags can repair. The completing fault can occur anywhere after the unfold, including in any layer inserted there. Flags must therefore catch the hooks themselves. A hook on a fold qubit that is a CNOT target is detected only by a two-qubit parity $Z_aZ_b$. No single-qubit flag measures such a parity without disturbing the check, and such placements exist in the $\delta=5$ double check:
\begin{proposition}[computer-assisted]\label{prop:flags}
No set of non-disturbing single-qubit Z- or X-type flags whose two couplings lie in the double check before the unpairing layer raises the real-T fault distance of the $\delta=5$ double check of Ref.~\cite{cultivation} above three. Two two-qubit flags raise it to four.
\end{proposition}
With the two flags, the logical error falls by 2.2 and 3.5 times at $p=10^{-3}$ and $5\times10^{-4}$ in the SI1000 model~\cite{si1000}, at a cost of 2 to 5\% in acceptance (see below). The hand-designed flags of Ref.~\cite{chan} also reach four in the full protocol, with 13 two-qubit flags, and five with a fourth stabilizer round~\cite{chan,wanzapirain}. Without that round, fault distance five requires flagging data errors that enter the check before its first T layer. The flag sets that our integer programs find for this grow to tens of flags and layers.

\paragraph*{Flag design.}
We now describe the flags and the program behind Proposition~\ref{prop:flags}. A Z-type flag on qubit $q$ couples $\mathrm{CX}_{q,f}$ at two layer boundaries and measures the flag qubit $f$ in the Z basis. It flips when the X part of the Pauli frame on $q$ differs at the two boundaries. It is non-disturbing when $Z_q$ at the two boundaries has a deterministic product in the skeleton, which we test by a tableau simulation. X-type flags are defined dually, and two-qubit flags couple to $Z_aZ_b$. The integer program chooses flags that flip for every physical representative of every malignant configuration. Constraints that involve faults of a flag, or of an inserted layer, are imposed only when that flag or layer is chosen. Every design is then re-certified exactly. Consider single-qubit flags with both couplings between the first T layer and the unpairing layer of the $\delta=5$ double check. Some representatives of the malignant configurations flip none of these candidate flags. They remain malignant in every flagged circuit, which proves Proposition~\ref{prop:flags}. With two-qubit flags, the program finds two flags, $Z_3Z_7$ and $Z_{32}Z_{33}$ (qubit labels of the published circuit), coupled over the whole fold and unfold. They give fault distance four. At uniform $p=5\times10^{-4}$, the flagged circuit has logical error $1.07(15)\times10^{-10}$ against $2.72(12)\times10^{-10}$ for the unflagged circuit, with acceptance 0.345 against 0.379. In SI1000 at $p=10^{-3}$ and $5\times10^{-4}$, it has $1.00(28)\times10^{-9}$ and $3.1(6)\times10^{-11}$ against $2.17(56)\times10^{-9}$ and $1.09(8)\times10^{-10}$, at acceptance 0.148 and 0.385 instead of 0.155 and 0.394. The remaining malignant configurations, of weight four, begin with X faults on two data qubits between the last stabilizer round and the first T layer. No flag inside the check can see them.

\section{Coherent completion and GHZ folds}\label{sm:mechanism}
This section turns Lemma~\ref{lem:completion} into a bound on the fault distance, explains what the bound asks of a check, and describes the GHZ-fold checks that we built at distance three, five and seven. Throughout, a circuit is a cultivation circuit on a color code of distance $\delta$, as in Lemma~\ref{lem:completion}.
\begin{corollary}\label{cor:hooks}
The fault distance of such a circuit is at most $s+\delta-|u|$ for every configuration of $s$ faults that leaves an undetected $X^u$ as in Lemma~\ref{lem:completion} immediately before the last T layer. In particular, it is at most $1+h$, where $h$ is the least number of faults that leave such an $X^u$ with $|u|=\delta-1$.
\end{corollary}
A check that keeps fault distance $\delta$ with real T gates must therefore be fault tolerant \emph{relative to the code}. That is, no $s$ undetected faults may place X on more than $s$, but fewer than $\delta$, qubits of a minimum-weight logical support before the last T layer. The proxy imposes no such condition, because the S layer turns these errors into Pauli errors with a detectable Z part. The condition is much weaker than ordinary fault tolerance of the check ancilla. At $\delta=3$ every pair of data qubits lies in a minimum-weight logical support. At $\delta=5$ only 4.6\% of the weight-four patterns do, and at $\delta=7$ only 0.04\% of the weight-six ones (Table~\ref{tab:cover}). Lower bounds on optimal fault-tolerant cat preparations~\cite{spidercat} therefore do not apply to real-T checks.

The double checks of Ref.~\cite{cultivation} measure the transversal check with a GHZ fold over data and partner qubits. A single X fault on a fold node spreads, after unfolding, to the data of a whole subtree. We call this a hook. For the circuits of Ref.~\cite{cultivation}, $h=1$ at $\delta=3$ and $h=2$ at $\delta=5$. Every malignant configuration has the form of Lemma~\ref{lem:completion} or of its second neighboring case, with the predicted acceptance $2^{-(\delta-1)}$ and bad weight (Table~\ref{tab:full}). The bound of Corollary~\ref{cor:hooks} is therefore attained.

Ref.~\cite{cultivation} gives circuits up to $\delta=5$, and how much fault distance is lost at larger distance was open~\cite{chan}. We therefore built GHZ-fold checks of the same structure on the $[[7,1,3]]$, $[[19,1,5]]$ and $[[37,1,7]]$ color codes, described below. For every fold tree we tried (three trees at $\delta=3$ and $\delta=5$, six at $\delta=7$, of depth one to seven) they have real-T fault distance two, three and four, against proxy fault distance three, five and at least five [Proposition~\ref{prop:fold}(ii)]. The real-T fault distance of the fold thus grows with the code distance, but at half its rate (Fig.~\ref{fig:fd}). Its failures are partly suppressed: at $\delta=7$, every malignant configuration of four faults is accepted with probability between $2^{-10}$ and $2^{-4}$, and the leading term of the probability of an accepted wrong output ranges from $0.011\,p^4$ to $15\,p^4$ over the six trees. The choice of tree costs nothing and changes this term by three orders of magnitude, but the loss of distance remains.

\paragraph*{GHZ-fold checks at $\delta=3$, $5$ and $7$.}
We now give the construction in detail. The circuits of Proposition~\ref{prop:fold} use the same surroundings as the cat checks of Sec.~\ref{sm:nn}, an ideally encoded input and flagged stabilizer rounds, and their one check round is built like the double check of Ref.~\cite{cultivation}. It consists of $n$ partners prepared in $|{+}\rangle$, the U$^\dagger$ layer, and $\mathrm{CX}$ from each partner to its data qubit. Then comes a fold $\mathrm{CX}(\text{parent},\text{child})$ over a spanning tree, leaves first, whose nodes are one representative (data qubit or partner) of each pair. The root is measured in the X basis and reset. Finally come the fold in reverse, the pairing CNOTs again, the U layer and X measurements of the partners. The root and the product of the partners read the check twice. The trees are spanning trees of the graph of data qubits that share a face. They are grown breadth-first from a central qubit or in random order. They are either unrestricted (depth one to three) or have at most two children per node (depth five to seven, as for the nearest-neighbor fold of Ref.~\cite{cultivation}), and their representatives are either data qubits or chosen at random. The detectors are all record parities that are deterministic in the noiseless proxy. The injected state is T$^\dagger|{+}\rangle$ at $\delta=3$ and $7$ and T$|{+}\rangle$ at $\delta=5$, the one for which Definition~\ref{def:dc} holds. At $\delta=3$ the three trees have 6, 12 and 12 malignant pairs, with leading terms $0.089p^2$, $0.57p^2$ and $1.0p^2$. At $\delta=5$ they have 68, 85 and 3 malignant triples, with $0.43p^3$, $0.69p^3$ and $0.076p^3$. At $\delta=3$ and $5$ these fault distances are those of the circuits of Ref.~\cite{cultivation}. At $\delta=7$ ($t=75$ T gates, affine dimension $\kappa=20$ of the Choi state, Sec.~\ref{sm:algorithm}) no tree has a harmful configuration of three faults. At weight four, the six trees have 15, 144, 80, 523, 362 and 352 distinct harmful errors, each realized by four fault classes whose exact detector patterns cancel. Each certificate takes 8 to 17 minutes. The proxy distances follow from the stim detector error model of the proxy circuit, with the same search.

The six fold trees of Proposition~\ref{prop:fold} are called central, central binary, binary 1, binary 2, binary 3 and random 1. \section{Verified cats and nearest-neighbor checks}\label{sm:nn}
This section gives the details behind Proposition~\ref{prop:vcat}. We first describe the verified-cat checks with all-to-all connectivity, then their nearest-neighbor versions on a square grid and what they cost, and finally a complete nearest-neighbor protocol and the circuits themselves.

\paragraph*{Verified cats.}
Each Shor-style check round~\cite{shor} first applies the U$^\dagger$ layer, where U is the transversal layer of T and T$^\dagger$ gates on the data. It then prepares a GHZ state, or cat state, on fresh qubits $c_q$ and verifies it by measuring all adjacent parities $Z_{c_q}Z_{c_{q+1}}$ before it touches the data. Next it couples the cat once to each data qubit by $\mathrm{CX}_{c_q,q}$ and measures the cat in the X basis. The parity of these outcomes is the check outcome. Finally the round applies the U layer. A fault in the cat preparation that spreads X over several cat qubits is caught by the verification, and every other fault reaches at most one data qubit. Flagged stabilizer rounds~\cite{chaoreichardt} surround each check round.

On the $[[19,1,5]]$ color code, the verified-cat check has fault distance exactly five with real T gates, both with one check round and with two. With one round, the probability of an accepted wrong output is $3452\,p^5+O(p^6)$ under uniform circuit noise. On the $[[37,1,7]]$ code, one round has fault distance at least five. Without verification, the same circuits have fault distance three at $\delta=5$ and four at $\delta=7$. On the $[[7,1,3]]$ code, the verified-cat check keeps fault distance three, where the double check of Ref.~\cite{cultivation} has two. The certificates exclude every harmful configuration of at most four faults, and the upper bounds come from Proposition~\ref{prop:singleton}. The S proxy does not distinguish the verified and the unverified circuits. With real T gates, their fault distances differ by two at $\delta=5$ (Table~\ref{tab:full}). All the unverified checks we certified, the GHZ folds at $\delta=3$, $5$ and $7$ and the unverified cat at $\delta=5$ and $7$, have fault distance $(\delta+1)/2$.

Verification has two prices. The first is acceptance, which is about half that of the unverified cat under uniform noise. The second is connectivity, since the cat check as written is not nearest-neighbor on a square grid. The nearest-neighbor checks below remove both costs. Acceptance also limits how far the distance can be raised. In our circuits, under uniform noise at $p=10^{-3}$, going from $\delta=5$ to $\delta=7$ lowers the acceptance from 5\% to 0.05\% for the GHZ fold, from 23\% to 1.5\% for the unverified cat and from 11\% to 0.09\% for the verified cat. At $p=5\times10^{-4}$ the values at $\delta=7$ are 2.0\%, 12\% and 2.9\% (Table~\ref{tab:acc}). Our circuits are not optimized for acceptance. For comparison, the published $\delta=5$ protocol accepts 14\% at $p=10^{-3}$. At $\delta=3$, fault distance three is also expensive in its coefficient. The verified cat has 2,405 malignant triples and a leading term $4.2\times10^3p^3$, which is larger than the $0.43p^2$ of the double check of Ref.~\cite{cultivation} for $p\gtrsim10^{-4}$.

We now move the cat check onto a square grid. There a cat state can be verified with the same economy if it is grown along a path of grid sites from the middle outward. A single X fault on a path qubit then either stays on that qubit or spreads to the part of the path beyond it, which contains one of the two ends. The parity $Z_aZ_b$ of the two ends $a$ and $b$, measured through an ancilla next to both, therefore detects every spread. Each data qubit sits next to its own path site and interacts with the check once (Fig.~\ref{fig:nn}). After the coupling we un-grow the cat: we apply the CNOTs that grew it in reverse order, so that the cat collapses onto its root, the site where the growth started. The check outcome is then a single X measurement of the root instead of the parity of the whole path. The other path qubits return to $|0\rangle$, and their measurements give further detectors. Faults in this step touch no data qubit. At $\delta=3$ the path runs around a rectangle and the ancilla closes it. At $\delta=5$ the path is folded into a U on three rows, with its arms on the rows $(x,0)$ and $(x,2)$. The middle row measures the rungs $Z_{(x,0)}Z_{(x,2)}$, each through one ancilla, and the diagonals $Z_{(x,0)}Z_{(x-1,2)}$, each through two adjacent ancillas. The rungs detect any spread confined to one arm of the U. The diagonals detect the equal spreads on both arms that two faults at mirror positions produce, and without them the ladder has fault distance four. Neither check needs stabilizer rounds: the code is checked only by the final ideal decoder, as in the circuits of Ref.~\cite{cultivation}.
With two-qubit gates only between neighbors of a square grid, the ring check on the $[[7,1,3]]$ code (17 qubits) and the ladder check on the $[[19,1,5]]$ code (46 qubits) have real-T fault distance three and five, equal to the code distance. Under uniform noise, the probability of an accepted wrong output is $3.6\,p^3$ and $12\,p^5$. At $\delta=3$ this is the same as with the S proxy. Under SI1000 noise it is $0.056\,p^3+30\,p^4$ at $\delta=3$, and its leading term at $\delta=5$ is $0.012\,p^5$. At $p=10^{-3}$ in SI1000 the checks accept 88\% and 39\% of runs. The ladder on the $[[37,1,7]]$ code (91 qubits) has fault distance at least six.
Without un-growing, every X measurement of the cat flips the check outcome when it fails, which happens with probability $5p$ in SI1000, and these measurements dominate the failures. The leading terms are then $6.9\,p^3$, with the end parity of the ring measured twice, and $43\,p^5$, at acceptance 89\% and 55\%. At each distance these versions have the highest acceptance and the smallest spacetime volume in Table~\ref{tab:cost}.

The grid checks also compare well with our other checks. At $\delta=3$ the ring beats every GHZ fold at every $p<10^{-2}$, with half the qubits and twice the acceptance of the best one. At $\delta=5$, fault distance five costs less than fault distance three: the ladder accepts four to six times more often than the unverified cat and 20 to 28 times more often than the best fold, and fails three orders of magnitude less often. Against the verified cat of Proposition~\ref{prop:vcat} it uses a third of the qubits and at most two thirds of the depth, accepts 13 to 18 times more often, and has a leading term 300 times smaller under uniform noise. Most of these differences come from the stabilizer rounds, which the ladder does not need. At $\delta=7$ the ladder accepts 7\% to 21\% of runs, where our checks with stabilizer rounds accept $3\times10^{-6}$ to $10^{-3}$. In SI1000 the minimal failures of the un-grown checks consist of faults of strength $p/10$, so the next order matters at $p=10^{-3}$, and at $\delta=3$ it adds the $30\,p^4$ above.

\paragraph*{A complete protocol.}
The checks above start from an ideally encoded state. On the grid, the state can also be prepared with every operation noisy. A T$^\dagger$ gate is applied to one data qubit in $|{+}\rangle$. The CNOTs of a non-fault-tolerant encoder are then relayed along the path: the control is copied onto its path site, spread along the segment that covers its targets, applied, and unspread. The encoder has hooks, single faults that leave X or Z on several data qubits. All six stabilizers are therefore measured, each with its own verified path cat, before three checks. This 17-qubit protocol has real-T fault distance three, equal to its proxy distance. Without the stabilizer measurements, or with only the Z stabilizers, it has fault distance two, because one more fault completes a Z hook of weight two into a logical Z that no check sees. The protocol pays for the stabilizer measurements. It has 107 layers, many of them measurement layers, during which every data qubit idles at $2p$ in SI1000. Its leading term is therefore $7.8\times10^3p^3$ at 11\% acceptance, which is ahead of the experiment's circuit for $p\lesssim3\times10^{-4}$ (Table~\ref{tab:cost}). Another route prepares $|0\rangle_L$ by measuring the X stabilizers and lets the first check project onto the T state, which needs no T gate. It also reaches fault distance three, at a higher cost. At $\delta=3$ on a grid, fault distance is thus no longer the obstacle. The obstacle is the cost of measuring stabilizers around the checks.

\paragraph*{Verified-cat checks.}
Here we give the circuits of Proposition~\ref{prop:vcat} and the numbers behind their certificates. At $\delta=5$ and $\delta=7$ the circuits start from an ideally encoded state, made from the injected state on one qubit and a CNOT encoder. A flagged stabilizer round follows, then one check round (or two, separated by a flagged stabilizer round), and then a last flagged stabilizer round. Uniform noise of strength $p$ acts after every gate, on resets and measurements and on idle live qubits, and the final readout is noiseless. The cat on $n$ qubits is prepared by a CNOT tree from one qubit in $|{+}\rangle$ and verified by the $n-1$ parities $Z_{c_q}Z_{c_{q+1}}$, each with its own ancilla. The check outcome is the parity of the $n$ X measurements of the cat. The injected state, T$|{+}\rangle$ at $\delta=5$ and T$^\dagger|{+}\rangle$ at $\delta=7$, is matched to the global parity of the T/T$^\dagger$ pattern. At $\delta=5$, the certificate of fault distance five for one round evaluates exactly the 56,319 distinct observable patterns of undetected configurations of four faults and finds none harmful. The enumeration at weight five finds 754,515 malignant configurations, with total weight $3.452\times10^{-12}$ at $p=10^{-3}$. Without verification, 99 malignant configurations of weight three have total weight $5.4\times10^{-10}$. For two rounds the weight-four certificate evaluates 3,220,729 distinct errors. At $\delta=7$ ($t=75$ T gates, affine dimension $\kappa=20$), the weight-four certificate of the verified cat evaluates 464,480 distinct errors and finds none harmful. The unverified cat has no harmful configuration of weight three. At weight four it has 38 distinct harmful errors. Each is realized by four fault classes whose exact detector patterns cancel, and each is accepted with probability $2^{-5}$, $2^{-6}$ or $2^{-7}$ relative to the fault-free run. At uniform $p=10^{-3}$ the acceptance at $\delta=5$ is 0.11 with verification and 0.23 without. In SI1000 at $p=5\times10^{-4}$ it is 0.17 and 0.30. The $\delta=3$ verified-cat circuit applies T to all seven data qubits in each layer. It injects T$^\dagger|{+}\rangle$ on one qubit before a non-fault-tolerant encoder, and it has two check rounds and three flagged stabilizer rounds. Its fault distance with real T gates is three, equal to its proxy distance, with 2,405 malignant configurations of weight three. With T$|{+}\rangle$ injected instead, its checks project. Definition~\ref{def:dc} then fails, with four accepted Z classes, and the fault distance is two.

\paragraph*{Nearest-neighbor checks.}
Here we give the circuits of Proposition~\ref{prop:nn}. They start from the same ideally encoded state as the cat checks above. They apply the U$^\dagger$ layer, prepare the cat on the path, verify it, and couple each data qubit once to its path site by a CNOT. They then un-grow the cat, measure the root in the X basis and the other path qubits in the Z basis, and apply the U layer. The final readout is noiseless, and there are no stabilizer rounds. Every two-qubit gate of the check acts on grid neighbors, and the construction asserts this. A CNOT counts as one two-qubit gate. On CZ hardware it carries two Hadamards, each with noise $p/10$ in SI1000. The path is grown from its middle site, one CNOT per arm per layer. In the ring ($\delta=3$) the path has nine sites, seven next to data and two corner bridges, and the ancilla next to both ends measures $Z_aZ_b$. One round takes 15 layers. The $30\,p^4$ term of Proposition~\ref{prop:nn} is the sum over all 9,340 malignant configurations of four faults. Without un-growing, with the cat read out by nine X measurements, the leading term is $16\,p^3$ in SI1000 ($30\,p^3$ with two check rounds), and $6.9\,p^3$ when the end parity is measured twice. In the ladder ($\delta=5$) the 19 path sites are the top row $(x,0)$, $x=0,\dots,7$, the turn $(8,0),(8,1),(8,2)$ and the bottom row $(x,2)$, $x=7,\dots,0$. Each is next to one data qubit, and the root is at $(8,1)$. The eight middle sites $(x,1)$ measure the eight rungs and, in pairs, the seven diagonals, with the odd and the even diagonals in two parallel layers. Each two-ancilla gadget uncomputes its second ancilla. One round takes 34 layers (24 without un-growing). The weight-four certificate evaluates 15,775 distinct errors in 7~s. The enumeration at weight five finds 85,464 malignant configurations. Without un-growing it finds 92,216, and in that case a second check round also keeps fault distance five, with 273,827 distinct errors at weight four. Without the diagonals the ladder has fault distance four, with nine malignant configurations of weight four. The $\delta=7$ ladder has 37 path sites ($17+3+17$), 17 middle ancillas and 91 qubits, and it injects T$^\dagger|{+}\rangle$. Its weight-four certificate evaluates 195,829 distinct errors in under three minutes. With the un-grown cat, we enumerated all configurations of five faults whose detector patterns cancel and found no harmful one, in a quarter of an hour on 14 cores.

\paragraph*{The complete nearest-neighbor protocol.}
The complete protocol of the main text uses the ring layout with every operation noisy. The data are reset. T$^\dagger$ is applied to the carrier, and H to the carrier and to the pivots of the encoder. Each data-data CNOT fan-out of the encoder is relayed. The path segment that covers the control and its targets is reset, and the control is copied onto its path site. The copy is spread along the segment and applied to the targets from their own path sites. It is then unspread and uncopied, and the segment is measured, with outcome deterministically zero. The three Z and the three X stabilizers are then measured, each with a verified path cat coupled to the four data qubits of the face. For the Z stabilizers the CNOTs are conjugated with Hadamards on the data. Three checks follow, with the cat read out by X measurements. The circuit has 17 qubits and 107 layers, and every two-qubit gate acts between grid neighbors. Its real-T and proxy fault distances are both three. It has 10,573 malignant configurations of weight three, with leading term $2.8\times10^3p^3$ under uniform noise and $7.8\times10^3p^3$ in SI1000, at acceptance 0.11 in SI1000 at $p=10^{-3}$. With two checks the leading term in SI1000 is $4.8\times10^4p^3$, and with un-grown cats the deeper circuit has $6.1\times10^4p^3$. If the Z stabilizers are measured instead by folding their parity along the path, weight-two Z hooks of the encoder go undetected and the fault distance is two. The alternative prepares $|0\rangle_L$ by measuring the X stabilizers with the path cat and lets the first check project. There, errors before the first check cause two problems. A Z error imitates a $Z_L$ frame, and an X error passes all T layers coherently. The X and Z stabilizers must therefore be measured again after the first check. The schedule X stabilizers, check, Z and X stabilizers, check, check has fault distance three and leading term $7.1\times10^4p^3$ in SI1000, at acceptance 0.05.

\section{A circuit run on hardware}\label{sm:google}
As an example of a circuit that has been run on hardware, this section analyzes the cultivation circuit of the experiment of Ref.~\cite{googlecultivation}. The experiment ran a $\delta=3$ color code on a square grid with nearest-neighbor CZ gates. It used injection, GHZ-fold checks with time-reversed unfolding, and error-correction cycles between checks, and it projected its logical error with the S proxy. We converted the published circuits~\cite{googlecultivation} into real-T circuits. Each physical $R_y(\pm\pi/4)$ rotation is written as T or T$^\dagger$ conjugated by the circuit's own $H_{YZ}$ gates, and the injection sign is chosen so that the fault-free check accepts with certainty. We added SI1000 noise, the model of the authors' simulations, and kept the virtual T gates noiseless.
\begin{proposition}[computer-assisted]\label{prop:google}
After one, two or three checks, the circuit of Ref.~\cite{googlecultivation} has real-T fault distance two, while its proxy fault distance is three. After two checks, each of its 20 malignant pairs contains a fault in the unfolding of the last check, and an ideal injection leaves the same 20 pairs.
\end{proposition}
The experiment is thus a GHZ fold with fault distance $(\delta+1)/2$, as Proposition~\ref{prop:fold} predicts. At the noise strength of the experiment, $p=2.3\times10^{-3}$, the two-check circuit accepts 22\% of runs. Its logical error is $2.4\times10^{-5}$ with real T gates, against $6.9\times10^{-6}$ with the proxy. The ratio grows from 3.1 at $p=3\times10^{-3}$ to 4.5 at $1.5\times10^{-3}$ and 6.1 at $10^{-3}$, because the leading term is $2.2\,p^2$ instead of order $p^3$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:google}). Projections of the experiment to lower noise along the $p^3$ slope of the proxy are therefore optimistic, by a factor that grows as $1/p$. On the same kind of grid and from the same ideal input, the two checks of the experiment have leading term $0.44\,p^2$, while one ring check of Proposition~\ref{prop:nn} has $0.056\,p^3+30\,p^4$. At $p=2.3\times10^{-3}$ the ring thus fails with about $10^{-3}$ times the probability, with 17 qubits instead of 14.

\paragraph*{Decoding the published circuits.}
Here we describe how we decode and simulate the published circuits. Ref.~\cite{googlecultivation} publishes its circuits as stim files. In these files every non-Clifford rotation is an identity tagged \texttt{T\_gate}, \texttt{T\_dagger\_gate} or \texttt{injection}, and the physical Hadamards that turn the $Y$ rotations into virtual $Z$ rotations are tagged \texttt{conjugate}. We replace the tagged identities by $H_{YZ}$, T and T$^\dagger$. We write the injection $R_y(\pi/4)|0\rangle$ as $\sqrt{Y}^\dagger$, T$^{\pm1}$ and $H_{YZ}$, which prepare the same state, with the sign for which the fault-free circuit accepts with certainty. The other sign halves the acceptance. The circuit of their Fig.~3 has 14 qubits, seven of them data qubits. It consists of injection, three error-correction cycles and three checks, the last of which is the kickback measurement. We cut it after the first, second or third check, or after the following cycle, and read out the six stabilizers and $Z_L$ noiselessly, as for the circuits of Ref.~\cite{cultivation}. The detectors are all deterministic record parities, so every run with a detection event is discarded, as in the experiment. SI1000 noise is applied layer by layer, with echo pulses treated as single-qubit gates, and layers that hold only T gates take no time. Every cut has real-T fault distance two with 20 malignant pairs and proxy fault distance three, with leading terms from $0.44\,p^2$ to $2.2\,p^2$. Making the injection and the first cycle noiseless leaves the 20 pairs and their weights unchanged. The single check of their Fig.~2, without cycles, has fault distance two both with real T gates and with the proxy. The logical errors of Fig.~\ref{fig:google} come from $1.2\times10^9$ to $1.1\times10^{10}$ samples per point. Ref.~\cite{googlecultivation} reports a logical error of $1$ to $2\times10^{-4}$ at 8\% retention for the kickback circuit with its own measurement. That number includes the error of the kickback readout and noise outside the Pauli model. Our numbers are those of the cultivated state before that readout.

\section{Open problems}\label{sm:open}
We close with five open problems.

\paragraph*{Stabilizer measurements on the grid.}
The nearest-neighbor checks of Proposition~\ref{prop:nn} keep the full distance from an ideally encoded state, and at distance three the complete protocol of Sec.~\ref{sm:nn} keeps it with every operation noisy. That protocol spends most of its layers on stabilizer measurements, during which the data idle. A cheaper way to measure the stabilizers around the checks on a square grid, or a preparation that needs fewer of them, would make full-distance cultivation competitive at the noise strengths of current hardware.

\paragraph*{Larger circuits.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:gauss} decides the collapse condition in polynomial time for circuits with deterministic checks, and this is how the circuits of Propositions~\ref{prop:gsj} and~\ref{prop:vcat} were certified. The cost of a certificate now lies in the search over fault configurations whose detector patterns cancel. At $\delta=7$ the searches at weight four take minutes, and those at weight five a quarter of an hour. The nearest-neighbor ladder at $\delta=7$ has no harmful configuration of five faults, and a search at weight six would decide whether it keeps the full distance.

\paragraph*{Lower bounds for real-T checks.}
By Corollary~\ref{cor:hooks}, a check that keeps the full distance with real T gates must be fault tolerant relative to the code, a condition much weaker than fault tolerance of the cat state itself (Table~\ref{tab:cover}). Known lower bounds on fault-tolerant cat preparation~\cite{spidercat} therefore do not apply, and we know of no lower bound on the number of two-qubit gates of such a check.

\paragraph*{Space-time cost at small distance.}
The circuit of Proposition~\ref{prop:t9} minimizes the number of T gates. Two questions remain open. The first is the least number of qubits, two-qubit gates or time steps at fault distance two. The second is how many T gates fault distance three needs, which is between ten~\cite{distillationpaper} and fifteen (Sec.~\ref{sm:t9}). By Theorem~\ref{thm:attain}, the second question is the distillation question of Ref.~\cite{distillationpaper} at order three. Integer programs over the fault map, as in Sec.~\ref{sm:t9}, apply to any family of circuits in which the fault map is linear in the design choices, such as orders of gates and positions of flags.

\paragraph*{Rates.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:branch} discards the acceptance probability, so bounds on the expected cost per accepted state need a different argument.
